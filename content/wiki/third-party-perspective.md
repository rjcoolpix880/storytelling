# Third-Party Perspective

**Summary**: When you need to challenge someone's thinking, bring in a respected outside voice (an expert, mentor, customer, data point, or story) instead of saying "I think." It turns "me vs. you" into something you're learning together, and makes people curious instead of defensive.

**Lenses**: [[storytelling]], [[executive]]

**Sources**: [How to Sell Your Ideas Better Than 99% of Your Colleagues](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kYv9N8F7l5o)

**Last updated**: 2026-10-21

---

Shannon Jenkins's second lesson from Don Draper's Kodak pitch (see [[how-to-sell-your-ideas]]): if you need to challenge somebody's thinking, don't make it you versus them.

## The Move

Kodak wants a pitch about technology. Don thinks they're wrong. Instead of saying so, he says: "My first job, I was in-house at a fur company with this old pro copywriter, Greek, named Teddy." Then he presents his idea as something Teddy taught him.

Jenkins: "Suddenly, Don isn't telling the client that they're wrong and he's right. He's sharing a lesson that he learned from somebody who knew this business long before he did."

- **Don't:** "I think we should do X."
- **Do:** "I learned from [Expert] that..."
- **Why:** It removes the personal friction of a "me vs. you" stance.

> [!conflict]- Disputed: Jenkins's own email says to own your view ("I recommend B, and here's why")
> In *Speak Like a Leader*, Jenkins says weak opinions and handing off the decision cost people trust in your judgement, and her fix is to state the recommendation yourself. See [[debate-own-it-or-borrow-it]].

## What Counts as a Third Party

Jenkins's list: "an expert, a customer, a mentor, a data point, a story." Her example of a data point, used to challenge a client's view without telling them they're wrong:

> "I recently came across a Stanford study showing that storytelling can be about seven times more memorable than facts alone."

(The statistic itself needs verification. See [[truth-vs-facts]].)

## Why It Works

1. **It changes the shape of the conversation.** A debate between you and them becomes a shared look at someone else's idea.
2. **It borrows authority.** Teddy "knew this business long before he did."
3. **It arrives as a story.** Don doesn't cite Teddy, he takes us to his first job. "In a few seconds, we have a person and a story that makes us curious about what this wise old advertising man taught him." Curiosity replaces defensiveness. See [[curiosity]].

## How This Relates to Other Pages

- **[[strong-opinions-loosely-held]].** The two pieces of advice look opposite: own your view vs. attribute it to someone else. See the debate page. Note that Don still has a strong opinion. He just doesn't put his name on it.
- **[[competence-and-warmth]].** A mentor story is a personal story (Don's first job) that shows humility. He was the junior learning from an old pro. It adds warmth without costing competence.
- **[[audience-journey]] (wiki synthesis).** Duarte casts the presenter as the mentor guiding the audience-hero. Don goes one step further: he brings in *his* mentor, and positions himself alongside the client as someone who learned the lesson too.
- **[[sounding-smart]].** Willis says persuasion is connection, not data. A third-party *data point* is still on Jenkins's list, but it works here as a way to defuse the disagreement, not as proof.
