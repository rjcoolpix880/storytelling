# Choosing the Right Story

**Summary**: Start from the message, not the story: decide what you want the audience to understand, believe, or remember, then look inward and outward for a story that supports it. For short business stories, test it against One Person, One Moment, One Change.

**Lenses**: [[storytelling]], [[executive]]

**Sources**: [Speak Better Than 99% of People: A Practical Guide to Storytelling](https://www.shannonjenkins.co/)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-25

---

Shannon Jenkins opens her written guide (see [[speak-better-than-99-percent]]) with one rule that comes before any technique: **the story serves the message.**

## Step 1: Get Clear on the Message

Ask:

> *What do I want my audience to understand, believe or remember?*

Only then look for a story. A great story that doesn't support the objective is the wrong story for this moment.

## Step 2: Test It: One Person. One Moment. One Change.

Jenkins's test for short business stories:

- **One person.** Someone the audience can follow, often you.
- **One moment.** A single scene, not a summary of six months. See [[five-second-moment]].
- **One change.** Something shifted: a decision, a realization, a mind changed.

Her CART example passes the test: one customer (the person), one meeting three weeks before launch (the moment), and the realization that the solution was built around the wrong problem (the change). See [[cart-framework]].

## Step 3: Search in Two Directions

| Look inward | Look outward |
|---|---|
| Your own experience and the people around you | Beyond your own experience |
| A conversation you still remember. A customer who said something unexpected. A project that went sideways. A decision you found difficult. A moment that changed the way you thought. | Your industry, another company, business, history, sport, science, or popular culture. Where can you see your message playing out somewhere else? |

**Looking inward** is where [[homework-for-life]] pays off: a daily log of moments is a ready-made archive to search once you know the message. Inward stories also do the work of [[competence-and-warmth]] by showing the human behind the expertise.

**Looking outward** is the only place in the wiki that covers borrowed stories. Jenkins's own video is an example: she makes her points through scenes from *Ted Lasso* (see [[how-to-tell-stories-better]]).

## Two Ways to Build a Story Bank

This comparison is the wiki's synthesis; no source sets the two against each other.

| | Message first (Jenkins) | Moments first ([[homework-for-life]]) |
|---|---|---|
| **Starting point** | What you need the audience to understand | What happened to you today |
| **Question asked** | Which story supports this point? | Which moment changed me? |
| **Where meaning comes from** | Chosen up front | Discovered afterwards |
| **Best for** | A talk or meeting with a specific objective | Building material before you need it |

They work together: collect moments daily, then pick from them once the message is set.

## How This Relates to Other Pages

- **The [[executive]] lens.** Deciding the message first is the same instinct as [[bluf-method]] and [[minto-pyramid-principle]]: know your bottom line before you speak. The difference is that Jenkins puts the message first in the *preparation*, not necessarily in the telling; the story itself still withholds its payoff (see [[curiosity]] and [[debate-answer-first-or-last]]).
- **The takeaway.** A story chosen to serve a message ends by landing that message. That's why Jenkins treats the takeaway as essential (see [[what-happened-vs-what-it-meant]]), a position the PAST source disputes (see [[debate-state-the-moral]]).
- **[[what-so-what-now-what]].** The message is the story's *So What*, decided before the *What* is chosen.
