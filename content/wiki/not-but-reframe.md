# The Not/But Reframe

**Summary**: To get people to accept a different idea, change how they see the thing before giving them the new answer. Say what it is *not*, then what it *is*, and land it with a short, memorable soundbite.

**Lenses**: [[storytelling]], [[executive]]

**Sources**: [How to Sell Your Ideas Better Than 99% of Your Colleagues](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kYv9N8F7l5o)

**Last updated**: 2026-10-21

---

Shannon Jenkins's fourth lesson from Don Draper's Kodak pitch (see [[how-to-sell-your-ideas]]): "If you want people to accept a different idea, don't just give them the new answer. Help them to see the thing differently first."

## The Line

> "This device isn't a spaceship. It's a time machine. Goes backwards and forwards. Takes us to a place where we ache to go again. It's not called the wheel. It's called the carousel. Lets us travel the way a child travels around and around and back home again."

Two Not/But pivots in a row:

| Not (how they see it) | But (how Don wants them to see it) |
|---|---|
| A spaceship (technology, the future) | A time machine (memory, the past) |
| The wheel (a mechanical part) | The carousel (a childhood ride, going home) |

Jenkins calls this the moment of mastery: the idea lands, and a piece of equipment becomes a vehicle for emotion.

## Why "Not" Comes First

The contrast does the reframing. Naming the old frame ("spaceship," "wheel") says out loud how the audience currently sees it, which makes the switch visible. Giving only the new name ("carousel") would just be a new label.

- **Don't (wiki illustration):** "We're calling it the Carousel."
- **Do:** "It's not called the wheel. It's called the carousel."

> [!conflict]- Disputed: BLUF and Minto say to lead with the answer
> The BLUF source and Barbara Minto put the conclusion first. Jenkins says to change how people see the problem before giving the new answer, and Don's reframe comes at the end of his pitch, after the Teddy story. This disagreement comes from different lenses: [[executive]] (the listener needs the answer to act) vs. [[storytelling]] (the audience has to be brought to see it before the answer lands). See [[debate-answer-first-or-last]].

## The Soundbite

Jenkins's term for a "short, sharp, catchy, and memorable sentence designed to make an idea stick." "It's not a spaceship. It's a time machine" is the line people will repeat after the meeting.

## How This Relates to Other Pages

- **[[what-is-vs-what-could-be]].** The Not/But pivot is the present-vs-future gap squeezed into one sentence: what the audience thinks it is, then what it could be.
- **[[surprise]].** "It's a time machine" is a small surprise: the audience expected one thing and got another. The lead-up (the Teddy story, the itch vs. nostalgia contrast) builds the stakes first, the way Van Neistat says a master saves the key fact.
- **[[the-expected-answer-trap]].** The reframe is how you deliver the less obvious answer, by first naming the obvious one.
- **[[minto-pyramid-principle]] (wiki note).** A soundbite plays a similar role to Minto's bottom line: one sentence that carries the whole argument. The difference is where it sits.
- **[[strategic-silence]].** After the soundbite lands, stop.
