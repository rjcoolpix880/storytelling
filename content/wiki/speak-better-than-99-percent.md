# Speak Better Than 99% of People

**Summary**: Shannon Jenkins's four-page written guide to business storytelling: start from the message, choose the right story, apply seven techniques, and shape the result with CART. It's the companion to her video [[how-to-tell-stories-better]], with new workplace examples in place of most of the *Ted Lasso* material.

**Lenses**: [[storytelling]], [[executive]]

**Sources**: [Speak Better Than 99% of People: A Practical Guide to Storytelling](https://www.shannonjenkins.co/)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-25

---

Shannon Jenkins is a leadership and storytelling trainer who works with senior leaders. This guide covers the same seven techniques and CART structure as her video (see [[how-to-tell-stories-better]]), but it's written for short business stories told by leaders, and it adds two steps the video skips: deciding on the message and choosing the story.

## The One Rule: The Story Serves the Message

Before anything else, get clear on your objective:

> *What do I want my audience to understand, believe or remember?*

Then look for a story that supports it. For short business stories, Jenkins gives a test: **One Person. One Moment. One Change.** See [[choosing-the-right-story]].

## How to Choose the Right Story

Once the message is clear, search in two directions:

| Look inward | Look outward |
|---|---|
| Your own experience and the people around you: a conversation you still remember, a customer who said something unexpected, a project that went sideways, a decision you found difficult, a moment that changed the way you thought | Beyond your own experience: your industry, another company, business, history, sport, science, or popular culture. Where can you see your message playing out somewhere else? |

See [[choosing-the-right-story]].

## The Seven Techniques

| # | Technique | In plain terms | Concept page |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Create curiosity | An unanswered question, a piece of information held back, or an unusual opening. Save the payoff "until it has earned its moment." | [[curiosity]] |
| 2 | Create tension | Uncertainty the audience wants resolved: *Will this work? Will the client say yes? Were we wrong? What happens next?* | [[tension]] |
| 3 | Zoom into a specific moment | Leave helicopter level. Start late, then use small amounts of dialogue, thought, reaction, and selective detail | [[five-second-moment]], [[right-amount-of-specificity]] |
| 4 | Change the pace | Slow down for the moment you want them to experience; speed up through what they only need to know | [[pacing]] |
| 5 | Introduce something unexpected | "Predictability kills attention." A surprising opening, detail, decision, or twist | [[surprise]] |
| 6 | Make it personal | Show the human behind the expertise; use your experience as a bridge to the audience | [[competence-and-warmth]] |
| 7 | Land with a takeaway | Never leave people wondering, "Why did you tell me that?" | [[what-happened-vs-what-it-meant]], [[cart-framework]] |

> [!conflict]- Disputed (technique 1): BLUF and Minto say to lead with the conclusion
> The BLUF source and Barbara Minto argue the answer should come first so the listener can act. This disagreement comes from different lenses: [[executive]] (the listener needs the answer to act) vs. [[storytelling]] (the payoff is what holds attention). See [[debate-answer-first-or-last]].

> [!conflict]- Disputed (technique 5): Van Neistat saves the surprise for the end
> Van Neistat says the master builds stakes and saves the surprise for the final beat; Jenkins also uses a surprising opening and a mid-story twist. See [[debate-surprise-placement]].

> [!conflict]- Disputed (technique 7): the PAST source says announcing the moral kills a story
> PAST says to end on your final Thought and stop talking, and lists "The moral of the story is..." as a story killer. See [[debate-state-the-moral]].

### Technique 5 in the guide: the Earl story, briefly

The guide retells the *Ted Lasso* press conference in one paragraph: Ted begins a tribute to a beloved dog that died on the pitch by talking about a dog that terrified him as a child. It seems unrelated and makes us think he doesn't like dogs. Then we learn the dog he feared became a dog he loved, "and the meaning of everything we've already heard changes." The full breakdown is in [[how-to-tell-stories-better]].

Jenkins's list of places to look for the unexpected:
- someone responded differently than expected
- your assumption turned out to be wrong
- the obvious solution failed
- something small changed your perspective
- new information changed the meaning of what came before

### Technique 6: what a personal story can be

- a conversation you still remember
- a mistake you made
- a moment you changed your mind
- a customer who surprised you
- something you noticed that changed how you lead

"The strongest personal stories contain something they can recognise in their own lives, work or decisions."

### Technique 7: two questions for finding the takeaway

1. *What did I believe before this happened?*
2. *What did I understand afterwards?*

Then turn that insight toward your audience.

## Do vs. Don't

### 1. Background first vs. a reason to keep listening (curiosity)
- **Don't (Instead of):** *"I grew up playing darts with my father, so when someone challenged me to a game, I knew I had a good chance of winning."*
- **Do (Open with something that makes them want to know how or why):** *"People have underestimated me my entire life. And for years, I couldn't understand why."*
- **Why:** The first version gives away the payoff in the first sentence. The second opens a question the listener wants answered. → [[curiosity]]

> [!conflict]- Disputed: PAST and the five-line story open by grounding the scene
> The PAST source says to state the where and when in the first sentence; the five-line story opens with the Situation (where, what, who). See [[debate-how-to-open]].

### 2. Reporting vs. story (tension)
- **Don't (Reporting):** *"There was a lot of resistance to the launch."*
- **Do (Story):** *"Three weeks before launch, my biggest customer looked across the table at me and said, 'This is never going to work.'"*
- **Why:** "Resistance" is abstract and already resolved. The story version has a deadline, a person with power, and a question the audience wants answered: will it work? → [[tension]]

### 3. Helicopter level vs. in the moment (zoom in)
- **Don't (Helicopter level):** *"We spent six months developing the product. We faced some challenges. Eventually, we changed our approach."*
- **Do (In the moment):** *"I'm sitting in the meeting when our sales director puts one number on the screen and says, 'I think we've been solving the wrong problem.'"*
- **Why:** "A story takes us somewhere." The helicopter version summarizes six months; the moment version puts the listener in one room at the turning point. → [[five-second-moment]]

### 4. The zoom-in toolkit
Start late: give only enough context to understand what is happening, then get to the pivotal scene. Then use small amounts of:
- **Dialogue:** *"She looked at me and said, 'This will never work here.'"*
- **Thought:** *"I remember thinking, I have absolutely no idea how we're going to fix this by Friday."*
- **Reaction:** *"He closed his laptop and pushed his chair back."*
- **Selective detail:** *"It was 6:30 in the morning when the email arrived."*

→ [[five-second-moment]], [[right-amount-of-specificity]]

### 5. Even pace vs. contrast (pacing)
- **Don't:** Give every part of the story the same amount of time.
- **Do:** Spend 30 seconds on one crucial conversation, then cover the following six months in one sentence.
- **Why:** "That contrast creates rhythm and keeps the story moving." The helicopter view is fine for the fast parts. → [[pacing]]

### 6. A feeling vs. a lesson (takeaway)
- **Don't (Avoid):** *"That was a really important experience for me."*
- **Do (Try):** *"That experience taught me that if we wait until an opportunity feels completely safe and obvious, we've probably waited too long."*
- **Why:** The first says the story mattered to the teller. The second says what it means, in a form the audience can apply to their own decisions. → [[what-happened-vs-what-it-meant]]

## CART: A Worked Business Example

"Once you have found the right story, use CART to shape it." Each step has guiding questions and an example line from the same story:

| Step | Guiding questions | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **C: Context** | Give me only what I need to understand the situation. Who? Where? What is happening? | *"Three weeks before launch, I'm sitting in a meeting with our biggest customer."* |
| **A: Action** | Take me into the pivotal moment. What was said? What did you think? What changed? | *"He closes the presentation, looks across the table and says, 'I don't think our customers will ever use this.'"* |
| **R: Result** | What happened because of that moment? A decision, a result, a failure, an opportunity, a realisation? | *"We stopped the launch, went back to customers and discovered we had designed the solution around the wrong problem."* |
| **T: Takeaway** | Why are you telling me this? What should your audience understand or do differently? | *"So when your customer challenges the idea you've spent six months building, don't defend it too quickly. Get curious. They may be showing you the problem you couldn't see."* |

See [[cart-framework]].

## How This Guide Differs From the Video

- **New:** the message-first rule, the One Person / One Moment / One Change test, and look inward vs. look outward ([[choosing-the-right-story]]).
- **New:** the zoom-in toolkit (start late; dialogue, thought, reaction, selective detail), the before/after takeaway questions, and a complete CART example set at work.
- **Reframed:** lesson 6 is now "Make it personal" rather than "Close the gap between competence and warmth," and adds the idea of a story as a bridge to the audience's own experience.
- **Dropped:** the "personal, not private" distinction and most of the *Ted Lasso* detail.
- **Softens one clash:** the guide's CART Context line opens with a time and place ("Three weeks before launch, I'm sitting in a meeting..."), which is close to PAST's rule for openings. See [[debate-how-to-open]].

## How This Source Fits With the Rest of the Wiki

- **Agrees** with [[past-storytelling-technique]]: Jenkins's zoom-in toolkit (dialogue, thought, reaction, selective detail) covers much of the same ground as PAST's Place, Action, Speech, and Thoughts.
- **Leans executive** more than the video does. "The story serves the message" puts the point first when *choosing* the story, which fits the [[executive]] lens even though the story itself still withholds its payoff.
- **Pairs with** Jenkins's email [[speak-like-a-leader]], which gives the executive counterpart: when answering a question, lead with the main point. The guide's "save the payoff" applies to stories.
- **Disagrees** with other sources in the same places as the video: [[debate-answer-first-or-last]], [[debate-surprise-placement]], [[debate-state-the-moral]], [[debate-how-to-open]].

## Exercises

None as such. The two takeaway questions and the CART guiding questions work as a checklist for drafting a story.
