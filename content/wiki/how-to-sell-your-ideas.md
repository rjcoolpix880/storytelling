# How to Sell Your Ideas Better Than 99% of Your Colleagues

**Summary**: Shannon Jenkins breaks down Don Draper's Kodak Carousel pitch from *Mad Men* to show why logic alone doesn't sell an idea, and five lessons for pitching: skip the expected answer, don't make it you versus them, make them feel it, reframe before you answer, and stop talking once it lands.

**Lenses**: [[storytelling]], [[executive]]

**Sources**: [How to Sell Your Ideas Better Than 99% of Your Colleagues](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kYv9N8F7l5o)

**Last updated**: 2026-10-21

---

Shannon Jenkins is a leadership communication trainer and storytelling coach (see also [[how-to-tell-stories-better]], [[speak-better-than-99-percent]], and [[speak-like-a-leader]]). In this 15-minute video she walks through one scene: Don Draper pitching Kodak's new slide projector.

## Core Thesis

"Logic is not the key ingredient of a winning pitch." Logic alone rarely creates buy-in. To influence people, make them *feel* the idea, deliver it with authority, and take a less obvious angle on the brief than the one you were handed.

## Three Techniques for Introducing an Idea (07:32)

1. **Speak with authority and conviction.** "His voice is slow and measured. He finishes his sentences on a neutral tone, never lifting his voice at the end as if he's asking for approval or permission." His hands stay mostly in the "zone of authority." See [[vocal-and-physical-authority]].
2. **Bring in an experienced expert's opinion instead of your own.** He presents the idea as something Teddy taught him. See [[third-party-perspective]].
3. **Deliver it through storytelling.** "In a few seconds, we have a person and a story that makes us curious about what this wise old advertising man taught him." See [[curiosity]].

> [!conflict]- Disputed (technique 1): Willis says charisma isn't polish or practiced gestures
> Rob Willis argues audiences respond to genuine belief, not rehearsed stage mechanics, and forgive a lack of polish. Jenkins treats tone and gesture as signals of authority, not charisma, so this is a difference of emphasis. See [[belief-vs-confidence]] and [[vocal-and-physical-authority]].

## Five Lessons From Don Draper (13:35)

| # | Lesson | Jenkins's explanation | Concept page |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Don't automatically give people the answer they expect | "Ask what everyone else is likely to say and see whether you can see something that they have missed." | [[the-expected-answer-trap]] |
| 2 | If you need to challenge somebody's thinking, don't make it you versus them | "Bring in another perspective, an expert, a customer, a mentor, a data point, a story to make people curious rather than defensive." | [[third-party-perspective]] |
| 3 | Don't rely only on rational, logical arguments (her "most important" lesson) | "Facts matter. Logic matters," but for people to embrace and act on an idea, "think about what you want them to feel, not just what you want them to think." | [[feel-not-just-think]] |
| 4 | If you want people to accept a different idea, don't just give them the new answer | "Help them to see the thing differently first." | [[not-but-reframe]] |
| 5 | When your idea has landed, stop talking | "Let them process the new concept. Let the silence do the rest of the work." | [[strategic-silence]] |

> [!conflict]- Disputed (lesson 2): Jenkins's own email says to state your view and own the recommendation
> In *Speak Like a Leader*, Jenkins says hedging and handing off the decision undercut your authority: "There are three options. I recommend B, and here's why." Here she says not to open a recommendation with "I think." See [[debate-own-it-or-borrow-it]].

> [!conflict]- Disputed (lesson 4): BLUF and Minto say to lead with the answer
> The BLUF source and Barbara Minto put the conclusion first, because busy listeners need it to act. Here Jenkins says to shift how people see the problem before giving the new answer, and Don holds his reframe until the end. This disagreement comes from different lenses: [[executive]] (the listener needs the answer to act) vs. [[storytelling]] (the audience has to be brought to see it before the answer lands). See [[debate-answer-first-or-last]].

## Do vs. Don't

**1. Challenging the brief**
- **Don't (what most people do):** Answer the brief literally and give the client exactly what they asked for ("reinventing the wheel").
- **Do (what Don did):** Realize every competitor is giving the same answer, and pivot to a deeper, less obvious angle.
- **Why:** It avoids a "race to the middle" and offers something nobody else will.

**2. Delivering a recommendation**
- **Don't:** "I think we should do X."
- **Do:** "I learned from [Expert] that..."
- **Why:** It removes the personal friction of a "me vs. you" stance.

**3. After the pitch**
- **Don't:** Get nervous, start over-explaining, or ask "Does that make sense?"
- **Do:** Stay silent and let the idea sink in.
- **Why:** It signals confidence and trust in the quality of the work.

## Smaller Points Worth Keeping

- **The calamine lotion metaphor.** Teddy's "itch" is a problem the audience already feels; the product is the lotion that soothes it. Don't just present an idea, position it as the relief for a specific, felt problem. This is Monroe's Need and Satisfaction steps in one image. See [[monroes-motivated-sequence]] and [[what-is-vs-what-could-be]].
- **Vulnerability as a catalyst.** Don uses his own family photos. Jenkins's point: you don't need to invent a story, you need to be willing to connect your own life to the idea you're selling. See [[competence-and-warmth]].
- **The Stanford study example.** Jenkins's sample line for challenging a client with outside data: "I recently came across a Stanford study showing that storytelling can be about seven times more memorable than facts alone." The statistic itself needs verification (see [[truth-vs-facts]]).

## Exercise

**Find the smarter answer.** Before building a pitch, ask: "Is there a less obvious but smarter answer? Do I see something in the problem that my client or boss may have missed?" See [[the-expected-answer-trap]].

## Key Terms

- **Nostalgia:** as Don defines it, "the pain from an old wound," "a twinge in your heart far more powerful than memory alone." (The literal Greek is closer to "homecoming" plus "pain"; see [[truth-vs-facts]].)
- **Zone of authority:** the space between your shoulders and hips where gestures should mostly stay. See [[vocal-and-physical-authority]].
- **Soundbite:** a short, sharp, memorable sentence that makes an idea stick. See [[not-but-reframe]].
