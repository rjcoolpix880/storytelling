# The 5-Line Story Framework

**Summary**: A minimal five-step scaffold for any story — Situation, Desire, Conflict, Change, Result — with each line serving a distinct structural function.

**Lenses**: [[storytelling]]

**Sources**: [The ONLY 5 Lines You Need To Tell Any Story](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NeTJRCycYXQ), [Why Trying to "Sound Smart" is Losing You the Room | Strategic Story Producer Rob Willis](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JBIeii_Qrrg), [How to Tell Stories Better Than 99% of People](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lghSjBl9yGM), [Speak Better Than 99% of People: A Practical Guide to Storytelling](https://www.shannonjenkins.co/)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-25

---

A story can be reduced to five lines. Each one does a specific job: orient the listener, give them something to want, create an obstacle, deliver the shift, and resolve it.

## The Five Lines

### 1. Situation — What's the starting point?
Establish where, what, and who. This grounds the listener before anything happens.

Maps directly to the Place and opening Action in [[past-storytelling-technique]]: a specific location and immediate physical context, no backstory dump.

> [!conflict]- Disputed: Willis and Jenkins open with a hook, not the setup
> Willis says to open with a high-stakes thought or question; Jenkins says to hold back the setup and open with an unanswered question. See [[debate-how-to-open]].

### 2. Desire — What does the character want?
State the goal. This is what sparks curiosity — the listener now has something to track. See [[desire]] for the full concept.

Nuance: desire creates both [[curiosity]] (*will they get it?*) and the stakes that make [[tension]] possible. Shannon Jenkins treats curiosity and tension as separate lessons, and neither strictly requires a stated desire. See [[curiosity]].

This is the step most absent from the other frameworks in this wiki. PAST starts in action; BLUF leads with a conclusion. Neither explicitly names the want that creates forward momentum.

### 3. Conflict — What gets in the way?
Introduce the obstacle or tension. Stories without conflict have nothing at stake.

Conflict is the main source of [[tension]]: desire supplies the stakes, conflict supplies the uncertainty. Jenkins's point applies here: at work, tension doesn't need drama, just a real open question.

Conflict is what makes the [[surprise]] land — it builds the emotional stakes the listener needs to feel the [[five-second-moment]] as a genuine shift rather than a neutral event. Van Neistat's master storyteller (see [[van-neistat-storytelling]]) instinctively builds this tension before revealing the surprise.

### 4. Change — What happens that shifts something?
This is the pivotal beat: the moment of growth, reversal, or realization.

Under different names, the storytelling sources in this wiki point to the same beat:
- [[five-second-moment]] (PAST framework)
- [[surprise]] (Van Neistat)
- Change (this source)
- "One Change" in Shannon Jenkins's test for a business story: One Person, One Moment, One Change (see [[choosing-the-right-story]])

All of them agree: this is the core of a story. Everything before it exists to deliver it.

### 5. Result — How does it end?
Resolve the story in a way that satisfies the listener. The source says the Result should "tie everything together." Tying the outcome back to the desire is one natural way to do that.

The result is where the story's emotional payoff lands. The source doesn't say whether "tying everything together" includes stating what the story meant, and other sources disagree sharply on that question: the PAST source says never announce the moral; Shannon Jenkins says the takeaway is the most important step. See [[debate-state-the-moral]].

## Delivering the Five Lines Live

Rob Willis makes a related point about performing a scaffold like this one out loud: "speak without a plan" — memorize the beats (Situation, Desire, Conflict, Change, Result) rather than a word-for-word script, and speak conversationally between them. Over-rehearsing a script makes delivery rigid; knowing the beats keeps it natural. See [[sounding-smart]].

## Relationship to SCQA

The [[scqa-framework]] (Situation → Complication → Question → Answer) used in [[minto-pyramid-principle]] and [[duarte-resonate]] is the argument-shaped sibling of this five-line scaffold. The mapping:

| Five-Line | SCQA |
|---|---|
| Situation | Situation |
| Desire | *(implicit in Question)* |
| Conflict | Complication |
| Change + Result | Answer |

The crucial difference is the explicit **Desire** step. In narrative, the character's want must be named so conflict registers as an obstacle. In argument, the audience usually supplies the question themselves from context, so SCQA leaves it implicit. Use the five-line scaffold when a person changes; use SCQA when a claim needs justification.

## Relationship to CART

Jenkins's [[cart-framework]] (Context, Action, Result, Takeaway) covers similar ground in fewer steps: Situation ≈ Context; Desire + Conflict + Change ≈ Action; Result ≈ Result. The difference is CART's fourth step, the Takeaway, which this framework doesn't name. Use the five lines to make sure the story has stakes and change; add a takeaway when the story is told to make a point at work.
