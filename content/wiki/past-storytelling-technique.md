# PAST Storytelling Technique

**Summary**: A four-pillar framework for telling vivid, emotionally resonant stories by zooming into a single moment of change.

**Sources**: [Give me 18min & I'll improve your storytelling skills by 183%](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YtkrIaONxu0)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-16

---

The PAST framework transforms "reporting" into "reliving." Rather than narrating events from a distance, it zooms into the [[five-second-moment]] — the instant you fundamentally changed or realized something — using four specific pillars.

---


## P — Place

Ground the story immediately by stating where and when it happened in the very first sentence. Avoid flowery scene-setting; a specific location and date is enough.

- **Do:** "Two weeks ago I'm sitting in my apartment in Amsterdam looking at my laptop."
- **Don't:** "Well, it was a warm Sunday afternoon. Birds were singing..."

A specific date makes the story feel authentic.

## A — Action

Lead with physical movement and active verbs. Skip background context dumps — put the listener inside the unfolding scene.

- **Do:** "Hey, I'm at the security check at the airport putting my bag in when someone calls my name."
- **Don't:** "I used to work at this company for about 3 years. It was a good company..."

Action creates a "mental movie." Physical verbs (walk, shout, open, close) are more effective than emotional summaries.

## S — Speech

Replace descriptions of conversations with the exact words spoken. Direct dialogue is the most effective way to bring a moment to life.

- **Do:** "My manager called me and said, 'Philipp, what the hell! You were all over the place in that presentation.'"
- **Don't:** "My manager was very upset with me."

You can reconstruct dialogue from memory — precision matters less than immediacy.

## T — Thoughts

Reveal your raw, unfiltered internal monologue. Don't analyze your emotions for the audience — give them your "live" thoughts as they occurred, even the awkward or petty ones.

- **Do:** "I thought, 'This is going to be terrible! They're going to tear my presentation apart.'"
- **Don't:** "I was nervous about the meeting."

This is what creates emotional connection — the listener hears what you were actually thinking, not a polished summary.

---

## How literal is the order?

PAST is not a rigid four-line script where you deliver Place, then Action, then Speech, then Thoughts as separate, sequential beats. The source's own "Fix" examples consistently merge Place and Action into a single opening sentence rather than stating them separately — e.g. "Two weeks ago I'm sitting in my apartment in Amsterdam looking at my laptop" is offered as the fix for Place, but it already contains action (sitting, looking). This isn't incidental: stating Place on its own risks recreating the "flowery description" trap the technique warns against, so Place gets folded into a line where you're already doing something.

Speech and Thoughts aren't batched separately either — as the five-second moment unfolds, dialogue and internal monologue are interleaved in whatever order they actually happened (dialogue, then a thought, then more dialogue), because the goal is to relive the moment chronologically, not to check off letters in sequence.

So the four letters are best read as **ingredients to make sure are present**, not four sequential paragraphs: ground the scene (place + time) fused with the first physical action in your opening line, then let the scene play forward in real time, dropping in exact dialogue and exact internal monologue at the moments they actually occurred.

## Connection to Other Frameworks

PAST shares a core philosophy with [[bluf-method]]: both are reactions against the tendency to dump complete information rather than crafting a targeted experience for the listener. See [[useful-vs-complete]].

Van Neistat's [[van-neistat-storytelling]] independently arrives at the same core: the [[five-second-moment]] is also the [[surprise]], and the job of every structural element is to deliver it. Van Neistat also makes explicit what PAST implies in the Speech pillar — that [[truth-vs-facts]] takes priority over factual precision when the two conflict.

The [[five-line-story]] framework offers a parallel scaffold: Situation maps to Place/Action, and Change maps directly to the Five-Second Moment. Its most distinct contribution is [[desire]] — the explicit naming of what the character wants, which PAST leaves implicit in the Action.
