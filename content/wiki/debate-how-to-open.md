# Debate: What Should the First Line Be?

**Summary**: Every source says to skip the warm-up, but they disagree on what replaces it: grounding the scene (PAST, five-line story), a hook or question (Willis, Jenkins), or a problem-focused attention-getter (Monroe).

**Sources**: [Give me 18min & I'll improve your storytelling skills by 183%](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YtkrIaONxu0), [The ONLY 5 Lines You Need To Tell Any Story](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NeTJRCycYXQ), [Why Trying to "Sound Smart" is Losing You the Room | Strategic Story Producer Rob Willis](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JBIeii_Qrrg), [How to Tell Stories Better Than 99% of People](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lghSjBl9yGM), [Monroe's Motivated Sequence | Public Speaking](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/wm-publicspeaking/chapter/monroes-motivated-sequence/)

## The question

Once the small talk is cut, what should the very first line of a story or talk do?

## Common ground

No source defends a warm-up. PAST lists "I have a story to tell you" as a [[story-killers|story killer]]. Willis says to cut "Hi, I'm so glad to be here, let's look at the agenda" (see [[cold-open]]).

## Position 1: PAST source and the five-line story — ground the scene first

- **PAST:** State the **where and when in the very first sentence**. The fix for "I have a story to tell you" is "just start with Place." See [[past-storytelling-technique]].
  - **Do:** "Two weeks ago I'm sitting in my apartment in Amsterdam looking at my laptop."
  - **Don't:** "Well, it was a warm Sunday afternoon. Birds were singing..."
- **Five-line story:** Line 1 is the Situation: a clear starting point with where, what, and who. See [[five-line-story]].

## Position 2: Rob Willis and Shannon Jenkins — open with a hook

- **Willis:** Drop the audience into the middle of a high-stakes thought or a compelling question. Start with the hook. See [[cold-open]].
- **Jenkins:** Open with something the audience wants to understand, such as an unanswered question or an unexpected opening, and hold back the setup. See [[curiosity]] and [[how-to-tell-stories-better]].
  - **Don't:** "When I was 10 years old, I used to play darts with my dad..." *(Note: this is a when-first opening, the kind PAST recommends.)*
  - **Do:** "Rupert, guys have underestimated me my entire life, and for years I never understood why."
  - **Unexpected opening:** Asked about Earl's death, Ted opens with being attacked by a dog at age 3.

## Position 3: Monroe — call attention to the problem

Step 1 of the motivated sequence is to get attention by pointing at the problem. The source's example opens with a statistic: "four out of five people do not get more than five quality hours of sleep per night." See [[monroes-motivated-sequence]].

## Where the sharpest clash is

Jenkins's "Don't" (*"When I was 10 years old..."*) is close to what PAST's rule produces: a time marker in the first sentence. PAST would say the time grounds the scene. Jenkins would say it gives away the setup and kills curiosity.

## The wiki's view (optional)

*This is synthesis, not a verdict.*

Some openings do both. Jenkins's own workplace examples ground the scene *and* hook: *"Three weeks before launch, my biggest customer looked across the table at me and said, 'This is never going to work.'"* It has a time, a place, and dialogue (PAST), plus stakes and an open question (Willis, Jenkins). The disagreement is sharpest when the grounding detail gives away where the story is going.
