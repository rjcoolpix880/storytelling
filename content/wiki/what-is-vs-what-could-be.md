# What Is vs. What Could Be

**Summary**: The oscillation between the present and a possible future is the underlying engine of persuasive communication — named explicitly by Duarte, executed as discrete steps by Monroe, and implicit in every story's desire/conflict structure.

**Sources**: [The Three Frameworks You Need to Create Powerful Presentations and Tell Compelling Stories](https://tomtunguz.com/storytelling-duarte/), [Monroe's Motivated Sequence | Public Speaking](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/wm-publicspeaking/chapter/monroes-motivated-sequence/)

**Last updated**: 2026-05-28

---

Nancy Duarte's central claim in *Resonate*: a persuasive presentation oscillates between the ordinary world of today ("what is") and the special world of the future ("what could be"). The gap between them is what motivates the audience to act.

This is not a presentation technique. It is the underlying mechanism that every persuasive framework in this wiki uses, named differently each time.

## Where the Pattern Shows Up

| Framework | "What is" | "What could be" |
|---|---|---|
| [[duarte-resonate]] sparkline | Present-tense observations | Future-tense vision |
| [[monroes-motivated-sequence]] | Need (step 2) | Visualization (step 4) |
| [[scqa-framework]] | Situation + Complication | Answer |
| [[five-line-story]] | Situation + Conflict | Change + Result |
| [[bluf-method]] | (the problem) | (the recommendation) |

Every framework here builds tension between a present state and a different possible state, then resolves it. The frameworks differ in *how often* they cycle through the gap — Monroe walks through it once across five steps; Duarte sustains the oscillation as a rhythm through the whole talk.

## Why Oscillation Beats a Single Contrast

Stating "here is the problem, here is the solution" once is the minimum. Sustaining the oscillation — repeatedly returning to what is and what could be throughout the presentation — keeps the audience locked into the gap. Each return reinforces the stakes and renews their interest in the resolution.

Duarte's sparklines for Steve Jobs's iPhone announcement and Martin Luther King's "I Have a Dream" both show this rhythm explicitly — the presenter cycles between present and future many times across the talk.

## Connection to Surprise and Desire

The gap is not just a persuasive device — it is the same engine that makes stories work.

- [[desire]] creates a gap between a character's current state and their wanted state. Conflict threatens to widen that gap; Change resolves it.
- [[surprise]] requires a gap between what the listener expected and what actually happened.

In storytelling, the gap is between expectation and reality. In persuasion, the gap is between present and future. Both rely on the same psychological mechanism: humans pay attention to *unresolved gaps* and lose interest when nothing is at stake.

## Practical Implications

- **Don't lead with the future alone.** A vision without a grounded "what is" feels detached.
- **Don't dwell in the present.** A problem without a credible alternative feels hopeless.
- **Return to the gap repeatedly.** Each return is an opportunity to reinforce stakes.
- **Make the gap concrete.** Abstract problems and abstract futures don't pull the audience in. Specifics on both sides do.
