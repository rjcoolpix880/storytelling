# Right Amount of Specificity

**Summary**: Give just enough concrete detail for the listener to see and feel the moment (a few well-chosen anchors) instead of either a vague summary or a flood of backstory.

**Lenses**: [[storytelling]]

**Sources**: [How to Tell Stories Better Than 99% of People](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lghSjBl9yGM), [Give me 18min & I'll improve your storytelling skills by 183%](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YtkrIaONxu0), [Van Neistat's Guide to Master Storytelling](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DyyjXFgM_6k), [Speak Better Than 99% of People: A Practical Guide to Storytelling](https://www.shannonjenkins.co/)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-25

---

Shannon Jenkins: you don't need loads of detail, just the right amount to help the audience see and feel the moment. See [[how-to-tell-stories-better]].

## Two Ways to Get It Wrong

| Failure | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Too vague** | *"Yes, I played darts a lot growing up."* | Nothing to picture; the moment doesn't land |
| **Too much** | A sprawling chronicle of every detail (Van Neistat's amateur walking through the entire hospital visit; see [[van-neistat-storytelling]]) | The important part gets buried |

## Getting It Right: Ted's Sentence

*"Every Sunday afternoon at a sports bar with my father from age 10 till I was 16, when he passed away."*

One sentence, four concrete anchors (**when:** Sunday afternoons; **where:** a sports bar; **who:** his father; **how long:** age 10 to 16), then one emotional anchor (his father's death). That's enough for the listener to see a ritual between a father and son, and to feel its loss, without slowing the story down.

**What makes a good detail:** markers of time, place, ritual, and relationship, each doing emotional or visual work. A useful test (a wiki suggestion): for each detail, ask whether it helps the listener *see* something or *feel* something. If neither, cut it.

In her written guide (see [[speak-better-than-99-percent]]), Jenkins's phrase is **small amounts**: use small amounts of dialogue, thought, reaction, and **selective detail**. Her example of a selective detail is a single time marker: *"It was 6:30 in the morning when the email arrived."* One detail sets the scene; the rest can stay out.

## The Nuance: Same Words, Different Job

The [[past-storytelling-technique]] uses this as its "don't" example for Place:

- **PAST's Don't:** *"Well, it was a warm Sunday afternoon. Birds were singing..."*

Ted's winning line also starts with "Sunday afternoon." The difference isn't the words, it's what they do. PAST's example is **scenery**: weather and birdsong that tell the listener nothing about the story. Ted's is a **ritual**: *every* Sunday, *with my father*. It carries the whole emotional weight of the reveal. Same kind of detail, opposite effect. Specificity is judged by function, not by type.

## How This Relates to Other Pages

- **[[useful-vs-complete]] and [[jit-context]].** Same logic, different measure. JIT asks what the listener needs to *do*; this asks what they need to *see and feel*. Both calibrate to the listener, not to the speaker's urge to be complete. One addition from Jenkins: "useful" doesn't mean minimal. Being too vague is a failure too.
- **[[pacing]].** Detail goes where the story slows down. Fast-moving bridge sections get almost none.
- **[[five-second-moment]].** Specificity is how you zoom into the moment. The helicopter view is the vague extreme.
- **[[zoom-in-method]].** That method adds depth for *understanding*, layer by layer. This adds detail for *experience*. See that page for the distinction.
- **[[truth-vs-facts]].** Details need to be concrete but may flex if minor. Ted's Whitman attribution is likely wrong, and the story still works.
- **[[mece-framework]].** MECE is exhaustive on purpose; it's a thinking tool. Storytelling detail is selective on purpose; it's a telling tool. Use MECE to organize what you know and specificity to decide what you say.
