# The Audience's Journey

**Summary**: Nancy Duarte's adaptation of the hero's journey for presentations — the audience is the protagonist and the presenter is the mentor, not the hero.

**Sources**: [The Three Frameworks You Need to Create Powerful Presentations and Tell Compelling Stories](https://tomtunguz.com/storytelling-duarte/), [How to Tell Stories Better Than 99% of People](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lghSjBl9yGM)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-22

---

Duarte argues that every persuasive presentation should follow the same twelve-step arc that great stories do, but with the audience cast as the hero. The presenter's role is to *guide* the audience through a journey from their ordinary world (the present) to a special world (the future).

The first five steps:

1. **Ordinary world** — describe the present as the audience knows it
2. **Special world** — contrast it with the future you are advocating for
3. **Call to adventure** — invite the audience to embark on the change

> [!conflict]- Disputed: Monroe saves the call to action for the end
> In Monroe's Motivated Sequence, the call to action is the final step, after Need, Satisfaction, and Visualization. Duarte issues the call third, near the start. See [[monroes-motivated-sequence]].
4. **Handle objections** — address the audience's immediate resistance
5. **Establish the mentor** — position the presenter as the guide, not the hero

The remaining steps follow the classic hero's-journey arc. Duarte demonstrates the parallel by mapping the framework onto Luke Skywalker in Star Wars.

## The Inverted Role

The most important move here is who plays the hero. Most presenters cast themselves (or their product) as the protagonist — *we built this, we solved this, look at us.* Duarte inverts this: the audience is the hero, and the presenter is Obi-Wan or Gandalf.

This changes everything downstream:

- The presentation is about the audience's transformation, not the presenter's accomplishments
- The future world is the audience's future, not the presenter's
- The "call to adventure" must feel like an opportunity *for the audience*, not a request *from the presenter*

## Connection to Other Frameworks

- The first two steps (ordinary world → special world) instantiate [[what-is-vs-what-could-be]]. The remaining steps sustain the gap across the whole talk.
- The "call to adventure" maps onto Monroe's Action step in [[monroes-motivated-sequence]], but the two place it differently (see the conflict note above). Whether Duarte also *revisits* the call later in the talk needs verification: the source says the contrast between present and future runs throughout, but doesn't say the call is repeated.
- The audience-as-hero framing is the persuasive parallel to [[jit-context]]'s listener-first principle: design around what the audience needs to experience, not what the presenter wants to say.

## Why "Handle Objections" Matters

Step 4 is the one most often skipped. Presenters who fail to address the audience's resistance early lose them before the resolution arrives. The audience's objections are not interruptions to the journey — they are part of the journey, and acknowledging them is what earns the mentor role.

## The Mentor Needs Warmth, Not Just Credentials

To be accepted as the mentor, the presenter has to be both credible and trusted. Shannon Jenkins's point about [[competence-and-warmth]] applies: presenters, especially leaders, tend to prove competence and neglect the human side. A well-chosen personal story helps.

There's a catch: a personal story can quietly turn the presenter back into the hero. Jenkins's example shows the fix. Ted Lasso's story about Hank ends by turning its meaning outward, to Earl and the people in the room. The personal story builds warmth; the takeaway gives the spotlight back to the audience.
