# Pacing

**Summary**: Vary the speed of a story. Slow down around the moments you want the audience to experience, and speed through information they only need in order to follow along.

**Sources**: [How to Tell Stories Better Than 99% of People](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lghSjBl9yGM)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-22

---

Pacing is Shannon Jenkins's fourth lesson (see [[how-to-tell-stories-better]]). Every part of a story falls into one of two kinds:

- **Moments to experience.** The turning point, the twist, the reflection. Slow down: more sentences, more detail, exact words.
- **Information to know.** Logistics, time jumps, background. Speed up: a sentence or two, just enough to follow.

## The Example: Ted and Hank

In the press conference about Earl, Ted slows almost to a crawl for being attacked as a toddler and for his final reflection on grief. In between, he compresses years of feeding, walking, adopting, and caring for Hank into a few quick sentences.

- **Don't:** Tell every stage at the same speed, giving bridge steps the same weight as turning points.
- **Do:** Linger on the attack and the reflection; fast-forward through the years in between.
- **Why:** Pace tells the audience what matters. Uniform pace tires listeners and dilutes the emotion.

## How to Decide What Gets Slowed Down

Ask of each part: *do I want them to feel this, or just know it?*

| Slow down (experience) | Speed up (know) |
|---|---|
| The [[five-second-moment]] where something changed | How you got there |
| The [[surprise]] or reveal | Time jumps ("years later...") |
| Peak [[tension]] (the last dart) | Setup the audience needs but won't remember |
| The meaning (see [[what-happened-vs-what-it-meant]]) | Operational details |

The slow parts get the detail. The fast parts get almost none. See [[right-amount-of-specificity]].

## The Nuance: Compress, Don't Cut

Pacing is related to [[jit-context]] and [[useful-vs-complete]], but it's a different move:

- **JIT context cuts.** If the listener doesn't need a piece of information to act, leave it out.
- **Pacing compresses.** The bridge material stays, just shortened. Ted can't skip Hank's years of neglect and care, because the twist and the meaning depend on them. He just tells them fast.

If you cut a bridge the story needs, the audience gets lost. If you tell it at full speed, they get bored. Compression is the middle path.

## The Nuance With PAST

The [[past-storytelling-technique]] is about slowing one moment way down, putting the listener inside it with place, action, speech, and thoughts. Pacing adds the other half: what to do with everything around that moment. PAST is the slow motion; pacing is the decision about where slow motion goes and how fast to move between those spots.

## Pacing at Different Scales

Several pages in the wiki deal with timing, at different scales:

| Scale | Idea | Page |
|---|---|---|
| Within a story (sentences) | Slow for experience, fast for information | Pacing (this page) |
| Across a talk (sections) | Alternate between the present and the future | [[duarte-resonate]] (the sparkline) |
| Whole talk (minutes) | Attention fades after about 18 minutes | [[18-minute-rule]] |
| Live delivery | Energy, pacing, and hooks fix drifting attention | [[captive-audience]] |

The common thread (a wiki synthesis): attention responds to variation. A steady pace, a flat talk with no oscillation, or a monologue that runs too long all lose people for the same reason.

## Writing vs. Delivery

Jenkins's pacing is mainly about how much space each part of the story gets. Delivery adds another layer (speaking speed, pauses), which Jenkins doesn't cover in detail. Rob Willis's four-second pause before answering a hard question, in [[sounding-smart]], is a delivery-level relative, though it's for tense conversations rather than stories.
