# Speak Like a Leader

**Summary**: Shannon Jenkins's newsletter email on executive presence: authority comes as much from what you stop doing as from what you add, and there are three habits to drop: weak opinions, waiting for permission to speak, and rambling.

**Lenses**: [[executive]]

**Sources**: [Speak Like a Leader](mailto:hello@shannonjenkins.co)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-25

---

Shannon Jenkins is a leadership and storytelling trainer. On the application form for one of her programs, every applicant ticked the same top goal: improve executive presence and authority. They want to "speak like a leader": project confidence, composure, and authority, so that people trust their judgement, take their ideas seriously, and act on their recommendations.

## Core Thesis

We usually focus on what to *add* to our communication. What we need to *let go of* matters just as much. Jenkins names three habits and asks the reader to check honestly whether they do them. If you do, that's the habit to drop. See [[executive-presence]].

## Habit 1: Weak Opinions

You have a clear view, but you wrap it in so many qualifiers that nobody hears it. Or you lay out the options and leave someone else to make the decision.

**How it sounds:**
- "I'm not sure, but..."
- "This might be a silly question..."
- "I just think maybe we could..."
- "I could be wrong, but..."
- "Does that make sense?"

By the end of the conversation, you've given people little reason to trust your judgement.

- **Don't:** Lay out the options and leave the decision to someone else.
- **Do (Try instead):** *"There are three options. I recommend B, and here's why."*
- **Principle:** Strong opinions, loosely held. State your view with conviction, and stay open to changing it if someone makes a better case.

See [[strong-opinions-loosely-held]].

## Habit 2: Waiting for Permission to Speak

You have something useful to say, so you wait to be asked. Nobody asks.

**How it looks:**
- Staying silent until someone calls on you
- Opening with "Sorry, can I just jump in..."
- Sharing your point with a colleague after the meeting instead of in it
- Hearing someone else say your idea and get the credit

- **Don't:** Wait to be called on, or apologize for speaking.
- **Do (Try instead):** Make your point when it's relevant. You don't need to dominate the conversation. One good question or well-timed comment can change the direction of a discussion.

See [[executive-presence]].

## Habit 3: Rambling and Overexplaining

Someone asks a simple question and you give them the full history.

**How it sounds:**
- "So, to give you some background..."
- "So you understand the context, let me explain..."
- A status update that starts in 2023
- Making the same point three different ways

- **Don't:** Start with the background and work up to the point.
- **Do (Try instead):** Lead with your main point, then add only the context people need to understand it.
- **Principle:** Speak less to say more.

This is [[bluf-method]] and [[jit-context]] in a single sentence. See also [[useful-vs-complete]].

> [!conflict]- Disputed: Van Neistat and Jenkins's own storytelling advice say to withhold the payoff
> In a story, Van Neistat says leading with the key fact is the amateur's mistake, and Jenkins herself says to save the payoff "until it has earned its moment." This disagreement comes from different lenses: [[executive]] (the listener needs the answer to act) vs. [[storytelling]] (the payoff is what holds attention). See [[debate-answer-first-or-last]].

## Your Action This Week

Pick one habit and decide before your next meeting what you'll do differently. Then pay attention to how people respond.

## How This Source Fits With the Rest of the Wiki

- **Puts Jenkins on both sides of a debate.** In her storytelling material (see [[how-to-tell-stories-better]] and [[speak-better-than-99-percent]]), Jenkins says to withhold the payoff. Here, for answers and status updates, she says to lead with the main point. That's the "different context" reading of [[debate-answer-first-or-last]], coming from one author.
- **"Does that make sense?"** is already on the PAST source's [[story-killers]] list. PAST says it breaks a story; Jenkins says it undercuts your authority. Two sources, same phrase, different reasons.
- **Conviction.** Strong opinions, loosely held fits Rob Willis's [[belief-vs-confidence]]. Note the vocabulary: Jenkins wants leaders to project "confidence," while Willis says charisma is "belief, not confidence." They mean different things by the word, not opposite things. See [[strong-opinions-loosely-held]].
- **The other half of Jenkins's leadership picture.** Her video and guide say leaders over-prove competence and need warmth ([[competence-and-warmth]]). This email is about the opposite failure: undercutting your own authority with hedges and silence.
- **Composure.** Willis's four-second pause before answering a hard question (in [[sounding-smart]]) is a composure tool, one of the three qualities Jenkins's applicants want to project.
