# CART Framework

**Summary**: Context, Action, Result, Takeaway, a four-part structure for turning a personal or workplace experience into a story with a point. The Takeaway is the part people skip most often.

**Sources**: [How to Tell Stories Better Than 99% of People](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lghSjBl9yGM)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-22

---

Shannon Jenkins offers CART as the fix for rambling stories with no clear purpose (see [[how-to-tell-stories-better]]). It turns a memory or work experience into a relevant takeaway instead of a point-by-point chronological report.

## The Four Parts

1. **C: Context.** The setting and background needed to understand the situation.
2. **A: Action.** The specific event, conflict, or choices made.
3. **R: Result.** The factual outcome.
4. **T: Takeaway.** The larger principle or meaning, turned back toward the audience's own situation. Jenkins says this is the part communicators most often leave out. See [[what-happened-vs-what-it-meant]].

> [!conflict]- Disputed (Takeaway): the PAST source says announcing the moral kills a story
> PAST says to end on your final Thought and stop talking, and lists "The moral of the story is..." as a story killer. See [[debate-state-the-moral]].

## Worked Example: Ted's Press Conference

| Part | In the Earl story |
|---|---|
| Context | Attacked by the neighbor's dog at 3; a childhood of fearing dogs |
| Action | The neighbor neglects the dog (Hank, the same one); Ted feeds him, walks him, adopts him |
| Result | Hank eventually dies |
| Takeaway | The thing that once made him cry from fear became the thing that made him cry from grief; such things help us get somewhere better. He hopes they did that for Earl |

## The Nuance: Ingredients, Not a Rigid Order

Jenkins presents CART as a structure, but her own examples don't follow it as a strict sequence. In the darts story, the Context (Ted's years playing with his father) is held back until the very end, because the context *is* the payoff. Telling it first would kill the [[curiosity]].

So, like [[past-storytelling-technique]] (see "How literal is the order?" on that page), CART works best as a **checklist of ingredients** to make sure are present, not four paragraphs in fixed order.

A related caution (a wiki synthesis): CART doesn't name [[tension]] or [[curiosity]]. Followed mechanically (context, then action, then result, then lesson), it can produce exactly the flat report with a moral attached that Jenkins warns against. Pair it with her other lessons: withhold the payoff, [[pacing]], and a scene-level [[five-second-moment]] in the Action.

## How CART Compares to Other Structures

| CART | [[five-line-story]] | [[scqa-framework]] | [[what-so-what-now-what]] | [[past-storytelling-technique]] |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Context | Situation | Situation | What | Place |
| Action | Desire + Conflict + Change | Complication (+ Question) | What | Action, Speech, Thoughts |
| Result | Result | Answer | What | *(the transformation)* |
| Takeaway | *(not named; the Result "ties everything together")* | *(the Answer is the point)* | So What (+ Now What) | *(none; end on the final thought and stop)* |

The takeaway is what CART adds. PAST deliberately ends without stating the point, which is the disagreement flagged above. The five-line story doesn't take a side. What/So What/Now What is the closest match. Its *So What* is the Takeaway, and CART's instruction to turn the takeaway back to the audience leans toward *Now What*.

## When to Use It

- Workplace examples and lessons learned, where the audience needs to know *why* you're telling them.
- Any story told in a meeting to make a point. That's Jenkins's context: leaders using stories to influence.
- For stories told purely for enjoyment, whether to state a takeaway at all is contested. See [[debate-state-the-moral]].
