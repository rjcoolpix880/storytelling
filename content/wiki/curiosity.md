# Curiosity

**Summary**: The listener's wish to *know* something: an unanswered question, a withheld piece of information, or a destination they want to reach. It is different from tension (caring how things turn out) and surprise (the unexpected answer).

**Sources**: [How to Tell Stories Better Than 99% of People](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lghSjBl9yGM), [The ONLY 5 Lines You Need To Tell Any Story](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NeTJRCycYXQ)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-22

---

Curiosity is Shannon Jenkins's first lesson (see [[how-to-tell-stories-better]]): give the audience something they want to understand. She names three forms:

- **An unanswered question.** "Why was Ted underestimated his whole life?"
- **A piece of information you're holding back.** Ted's darts history.
- **A destination the audience wants you to take them to.** They sense the story is going somewhere and want to find out where.

## The Core Rule: Withhold the Payoff

Give the audience what they need to keep following you, but don't give them the payoff until you're ready for it.

- **Don't:** *"I grew up playing darts with my father, so when this guy challenged me to a game, I decided to play dumb."* The trick is explained before it's performed.
- **Do:** Ted asks the question nobody asked (*"Have you played a lot of darts, Ted?"*), lets it hang, and answers only on the final throw.

> [!conflict]- Disputed: BLUF and Minto say to lead with the conclusion
> The BLUF source and Barbara Minto argue the answer should come first, because busy listeners need it to act and a clear headline invites their questions. See [[debate-answer-first-or-last]].

Withholding is not the same as being vague. Ted gives plenty of concrete detail along the way (the drive to school, the quote on the wall). He holds back only the *answer*. See [[right-amount-of-specificity]].

## The Nuance: Curiosity, Tension, Desire, and Surprise

These four ideas overlap and are easy to confuse. Each does a different job:

| Concept | What the listener feels | The question in their head | Darts example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Curiosity** | "I want to know." | *Why? How? What's the connection?* | Why was Ted always underestimated? |
| **[[tension]]** | "I care how this turns out." | *Will it work out? What's at risk?* | Will he hit the bullseye? Will Rupert get to pick the lineup? |
| **[[desire]]** | "I know what they want." | *Will they get it?* | Ted wants to win the bet |
| **[[surprise]]** | "I didn't see that coming." | *Oh!* | He played every Sunday with his father |

How they relate:

- **Curiosity can exist without stakes.** A riddle makes you curious even though nothing depends on the answer. Tension needs something at risk.
- **Tension can exist without curiosity.** In a crisis everyone knows exactly what's happening; they just don't know how it ends.
- **Desire is one engine for both.** Naming what someone wants raises a question (curiosity) and creates something to lose (tension). But it isn't the only engine. Ted's darts hook creates curiosity from a puzzle about himself, not from a stated want.
- **Surprise is one way to answer curiosity**, and a good surprise can *restart* it. When Ted reveals that the neglected dog was Hank, the audience's assumption collapses and they no longer know where the story is going.

**Where the source blurs this:** Jenkins lists curiosity and tension as separate lessons, but her own definition of tension ("creating curiosity in your audience's mind that they then want to resolve") folds the two together. The four-way split above is the wiki's synthesis, not something the source states. It's kept because it's useful: when a story feels flat, it helps to ask whether it's missing a question (curiosity) or missing stakes (tension).

## Ways to Create Curiosity

- **Open with a question you don't answer yet.** Ted's "guys have underestimated me my entire life."
- **Open with something unexpected.** Start as far as possible from what the audience expects. Asked about Earl's death, Ted begins with being attacked by a dog as a toddler. The gap between the prompt and the opening makes the audience work out the connection. See [[cold-open]] and [[surprise]].
- **Pose the question the listener didn't know to ask.** "Have you played a lot of darts, Ted?"
- **State a want.** See [[desire]].
- **Make the question explicit in an argument.** The Question beat in [[scqa-framework]] is curiosity written into the structure.

> [!conflict]- Disputed: PAST and the five-line story open by grounding the scene
> The PAST source says to state the where and when in the first sentence; the five-line story opens with the Situation (where, what, who). See [[debate-how-to-open]].
