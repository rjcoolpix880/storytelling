# Strategic Silence

**Summary**: Once your idea has landed, stop talking. Silence shows confidence in the work and gives the audience room to process; filling it with more explanation dilutes the impact.

**Lenses**: [[storytelling]], [[executive]]

**Sources**: [How to Sell Your Ideas Better Than 99% of Your Colleagues](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kYv9N8F7l5o), [Why Trying to "Sound Smart" is Losing You the Room | Strategic Story Producer Rob Willis](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JBIeii_Qrrg), [Give me 18min & I'll improve your storytelling skills by 183%](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YtkrIaONxu0)

**Last updated**: 2026-10-21

---

Shannon Jenkins's fifth lesson from Don Draper's Kodak pitch (see [[how-to-sell-your-ideas]]): "When your idea has landed, stop talking. Let them process the new concept. Let the silence do the rest of the work."

"Used well, silence is true strength."

## The Moment

After the carousel line, Don doesn't say another word. Jenkins's reading: he has already said everything he needed to say, and adding more would only dilute it. He resists the urge many professionals feel when there's no immediate feedback: to get nervous and start over-explaining, or probe to see how it went.

- **Don't:** Get nervous, start over-explaining, or ask "Does that make sense?"
- **Do:** Stay silent and let the idea sink in.
- **Why:** It signals confidence and trust in the quality of the work.

## Three Kinds of Silence in the Wiki

Silence shows up in several sources, doing different jobs:

| Silence | When | What it does | Page |
|---|---|---|---|
| **After the idea lands** (Jenkins) | You've finished the pitch | Lets them process; shows confidence | This page |
| **Before answering** (Willis's four-second rule) | A tense moment or a hard question | Prevents a defensive reaction; projects quiet confidence | [[sounding-smart]] |
| **Instead of speaking up** (the habit Jenkins says to drop) | You have something useful to say and wait to be asked | Costs you influence and credit | [[executive-presence]] |

**Not a contradiction (wiki note).** Jenkins's email tells people to stop staying silent in meetings, and this video tells them to stay silent. The first is silence instead of making your point; the second is silence after you've made it.

## The Same Idea in Stories

The PAST source's fix for "The moral of the story is...": end on your final thought or transformation, then stop (see [[story-killers]]). And "Does that make sense?" now appears in three sources: PAST (it breaks immersion), Jenkins's email (it undercuts authority), and this video (it's the nervous question that fills a silence you should have left alone).

## How This Relates to Other Pages

- **[[useful-vs-complete]].** Adding more after the idea lands is the over-explaining failure at the very end, when it does the most damage.
- **[[pacing]].** Pausing after the key line is a delivery-level version of slowing down for the moment that matters.
