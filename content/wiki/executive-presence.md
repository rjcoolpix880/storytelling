# Executive Presence

**Summary**: Speaking so that people trust your judgement, take your ideas seriously, and act on your recommendations. Shannon Jenkins argues it comes as much from dropping habits (weak opinions, waiting for permission, rambling) as from adding skills.

**Lenses**: [[executive]]

**Sources**: [Speak Like a Leader](mailto:hello@shannonjenkins.co), [Why Trying to "Sound Smart" is Losing You the Room | Strategic Story Producer Rob Willis](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JBIeii_Qrrg)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-25

---

Executive presence is the top goal of the leaders Shannon Jenkins trains: every applicant to one of her programs ticked it as their number one goal (see [[speak-like-a-leader]]). Her definition is about effect, not style: projecting **confidence, composure, and authority** so that people:

- trust your judgement,
- take your ideas seriously, and
- act on your recommendations.

## Presence by Subtraction

Jenkins's key move: most advice is about what to add. What you let go of matters just as much. She names three habits to drop.

| Habit to drop | How it shows up | Try instead | Page |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Weak opinions** | "I'm not sure, but...", "I could be wrong, but...", "Does that make sense?"; laying out options and leaving the decision to others | *"There are three options. I recommend B, and here's why."* | [[strong-opinions-loosely-held]] |
| **Waiting for permission to speak** | Staying silent until called on; "Sorry, can I just jump in..."; making your point to a colleague after the meeting; hearing someone else say your idea and get the credit | Make your point when it's relevant. One good question or well-timed comment can change the direction of a discussion | This page (below) |
| **Rambling and overexplaining** | "So, to give you some background..."; a status update that starts in 2023; making the same point three different ways | Lead with your main point, then add only the context people need. "Speak less to say more." | [[bluf-method]], [[jit-context]] |

> [!conflict]- Disputed (rambling row): Van Neistat and Jenkins's own storytelling advice say to withhold the payoff
> In a story, Van Neistat says leading with the key fact is the amateur's mistake, and Jenkins herself says to save the payoff until it has earned its moment. This disagreement comes from different lenses: [[executive]] (the listener needs the answer to act) vs. [[storytelling]] (the payoff is what holds attention). See [[debate-answer-first-or-last]].

## Speaking Up Without Taking Over

Jenkins's fix for waiting for permission is modest: you don't need to dominate, just make your point when it's relevant. Two pages in the wiki shape how to do that well:

- **Listen first.** Rob Willis's "ditch fix-it mode" says to stop drafting your reply while the other person is still talking (see [[sounding-smart]]). Speaking up at the right moment depends on actually hearing the discussion.
- **Meetings are for discussion.** Willis's [[three-to-one-ratio]] (three parts discussion to one part presentation) only works if people contribute. Waiting to be asked is how a discussion turns back into a monologue.

## Composure

Of Jenkins's three qualities, composure is the one the email doesn't give a technique for. Willis's **four-second rule** fits: when a conversation turns tense or you're asked a hard question, pause four full seconds before responding. It prevents defensive reactions and "projects quiet confidence." See [[sounding-smart]].

## How This Relates to Other Pages

- **[[competence-and-warmth]].** Jenkins's storytelling material warns that leaders over-prove competence and neglect warmth. Presence is about the opposite failure: undercutting your own competence. Hedges like "This might be a silly question..." may feel humble or warm, but Jenkins says they cost trust in your judgement. The wiki's reading: warmth comes from a well-chosen personal story, not from apologizing for your views.
- **[[belief-vs-confidence]].** Willis says charisma is "belief, not confidence." Jenkins wants leaders to project "confidence." The words clash, but the ideas don't: Willis's "confidence" means polish and rehearsed delivery; Jenkins's means conviction in your own view, which is close to what Willis calls belief.
- **[[story-killers]].** The PAST source's list of phrases that kill a story overlaps with Jenkins's hedges: both flag "Does that make sense?" and pre-emptive apologies ("I'm not a good storyteller" / "Sorry, can I just jump in...").
- **[[useful-vs-complete]].** Rambling is the useful-vs-complete failure in a meeting. Willis's Knowledge Deficit Trap names the same urge: over-explaining to sound credible.

**A nuance on repetition (wiki synthesis):** Jenkins lists "making the same point three different ways" as rambling. Nancy Duarte recommends returning to the gap between the present and the future again and again across a talk (see [[what-is-vs-what-could-be]]). These aren't in conflict: Jenkins is describing an answer in a meeting, and Duarte's returns each bring new material to a whole presentation.
