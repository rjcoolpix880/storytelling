# Competence and Warmth

**Summary**: Leaders tend to prove competence at the expense of warmth. A well-chosen personal (not private) story shows the human behind the expertise, so people can trust, connect with, and follow them.

**Sources**: [How to Tell Stories Better Than 99% of People](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lghSjBl9yGM)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-22

---

Shannon Jenkins's sixth lesson (see [[how-to-tell-stories-better]]): use personal stories to close the gap between competence and warmth.

## The Gap

Leaders often overemphasize their competence and credibility and neglect the human side. That creates distance. Jenkins's point is that people need **both**:

- **Competence:** believing you know what you're doing.
- **Warmth:** feeling they can trust you, connect with you, and follow your direction.

A leader who only shows competence is respected but not followed.

## Personal, Not Private

Jenkins: you don't need to overshare, and personal does not mean private.

| | Personal | Private |
|---|---|---|
| **What it reveals** | Values, rituals, emotions | Intimate matters that serve no purpose in the story |
| **Effect** | Builds warmth and identification | Makes the audience uncomfortable; shifts focus to the teller |
| **Example** | Ted's Sunday darts ritual with his father, and his father's death | (Jenkins gives no example of the private side) |

A useful test (a wiki suggestion, not from the source): does this detail serve the story's point *and* the listener? Would I be comfortable having it repeated?

## One Reveal, Both Qualities

The darts payoff shows competence and warmth in the same sentence. "Every Sunday afternoon at a sports bar with my father from age 10 till I was 16" proves mastery (years of practice). "When he passed away" shows grief. The audience ends up respecting him *and* feeling for him at once.

## The Nuance With Other Pages

**[[belief-vs-confidence]].** Rob Willis argues charisma comes from conviction, not polish. Jenkins argues trust comes from warmth, not just credentials. Both say that looking impressive isn't what wins a room, but they name different missing ingredients:

| | Belief vs. Confidence (Willis) | Competence and Warmth (Jenkins) |
|---|---|---|
| **The trap** | Relying on polish and rehearsed delivery | Relying on credentials and expertise |
| **What's missing** | Genuine conviction about the idea | Visible humanity |
| **Your relationship to...** | The *idea* | The *audience* |

**[[sounding-smart]].** Willis's Knowledge Deficit Trap (over-explaining to sound smart) is the competence-heavy failure applied to *content*. The competence–warmth gap is the same failure applied to how you *present yourself*.

**[[audience-journey]].** Duarte casts the presenter as the mentor, not the hero. A mentor needs both qualities: credible enough to guide, warm enough to be trusted. But there's a risk: a personal story can accidentally make the presenter the hero. The fix is the one Ted uses: turn the meaning outward, to the audience (and to Earl), through the takeaway. See [[what-happened-vs-what-it-meant]].
