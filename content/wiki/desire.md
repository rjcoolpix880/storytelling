# Desire

**Summary**: The character's stated goal in a story — the element that sparks audience curiosity and gives conflict something to threaten.

**Sources**: [The ONLY 5 Lines You Need To Tell Any Story](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NeTJRCycYXQ), [How to Tell Stories Better Than 99% of People](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lghSjBl9yGM)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-22

---

In the [[five-line-story]] framework, Desire is the second line: what does the character want? Stating the goal does two things at once — it shows the character's motivation and activates audience curiosity. The listener now has something to track: will they get it?

## Why It Matters Structurally

Desire creates the logic that holds the rest of the story together:

- **Conflict** only registers as an obstacle because there's a desire it threatens
- **Change** only reads as growth or reversal because the listener knows what was wanted
- **Result** only satisfies because it resolves whether the desire was met

Without a clear desire, conflict is just an event and change is just a transition.

## Desire and Curiosity

Explicitly stating what a character wants is a curiosity mechanism. The listener can't help but wonder whether the desire will be achieved — and how. This is distinct from the [[surprise]], which delivers an unexpected beat. Desire creates a *question*; surprise delivers an *unexpected answer*.

**The nuance (from Jenkins):** Shannon Jenkins treats curiosity and tension as two separate lessons (see [[how-to-tell-stories-better]]). Desire feeds both. Stating a want raises a question (*will they get it?*), which is [[curiosity]], and creates something that can be lost, which gives [[tension]] its stakes. But desire isn't the only source of either. Ted Lasso's darts story creates curiosity from a puzzle ("why have people always underestimated me?") without stating a goal first. Desire is one reliable engine, not a requirement. See [[curiosity]] for how the four ideas (curiosity, tension, desire, surprise) fit together.

## Desire in the Other Frameworks

Neither [[past-storytelling-technique]] nor the [[bluf-method]] explicitly name desire, but it is implicit in both:

- In PAST, the Action pillar drops the listener into motion — but the motion is purposeful. What the person is trying to do (even if unstated) creates the stakes that make the [[five-second-moment]] feel like a disruption.
- In BLUF, the speaker's "ask" at the top of a communication is essentially their desire — what they want the listener to do. JIT Context and Zoom-In then serve that stated goal.

Naming desire explicitly (as this framework does) makes a common implicit element visible and intentional.
