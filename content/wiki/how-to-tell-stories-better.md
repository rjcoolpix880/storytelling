# How to Tell Stories Better Than 99% of People

**Summary**: Shannon Jenkins uses two *Ted Lasso* scenes to show seven moves that turn a report into a story (curiosity, tension, zooming in, pacing, the unexpected, warmth, and a takeaway), plus the CART structure that ties them together.

**Sources**: [How to Tell Stories Better Than 99% of People](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lghSjBl9yGM)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-22

---

Shannon Jenkins is a leadership and storytelling trainer (clients include Nestlé, Nespresso, and Google). Her audience is leaders, and her framing is practical: storytelling is how a leader disarms skepticism, makes an idea memorable, and moves people to act.

## Core Thesis

Weak communicators *report*: summaries, chronology, credentials. Strong ones hook attention with an unresolved question, give the right amount of detail, lead the listener toward an assumption and then overturn it, vary the pace, say what the events *meant*, and balance competence with human warmth.

## The CART Framework

**Context → Action → Result → Takeaway.** Jenkins says the Takeaway is the component communicators most often leave out. See [[cart-framework]].

## The Seven Lessons

| # | Lesson | In plain terms | Concept page |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Create curiosity | Give the audience something they want to understand: an unanswered question, a withheld piece of information, a destination | [[curiosity]] |
| 2 | Create tension | Give them a reason to care what happens next: something uncertain and something important at stake | [[tension]] |
| 3 | Zoom in to a specific moment | Not the "helicopter view" of everything that happened, but the specific meeting, conversation, or moment where something changed | [[five-second-moment]] |
| 4 | Change the pace | Slow down around moments you want them to experience; speed through what they simply need to know | [[pacing]] |
| 5 | Introduce something unexpected | A twist, a surprising detail, or an unexpected opening | [[surprise]] |
| 6 | Close the gap between competence and warmth | A well-chosen personal (not private) detail shows the human behind the expertise | [[competence-and-warmth]] |
| 7 | Land with a takeaway | Don't leave them wondering why you told the story: what did it mean, what changed? | [[what-happened-vs-what-it-meant]], [[cart-framework]] |

> [!conflict]- Disputed (lesson 5): Van Neistat saves the surprise for the end
> Van Neistat says the master builds stakes and saves the surprise for the final beat; Jenkins also places the unexpected at the start and in the middle. See [[debate-surprise-placement]].

> [!conflict]- Disputed (lesson 7): the PAST source says announcing the moral kills a story
> PAST says to end on your final Thought and stop talking, and lists "The moral of the story is..." as a story killer. See [[debate-state-the-moral]].

A principle that runs through lessons 3–5: use the **right amount of specificity**, not the most. See [[right-amount-of-specificity]].

**A note on lessons 1 and 2:** Jenkins lists curiosity and tension as separate lessons, but her own definition of tension ("creating curiosity in your audience's mind that they then want to resolve") blurs them. The wiki keeps them as separate pages because the difference is useful in practice. See [[curiosity]] and [[tension]].

## Example 1: The Darts Scene

**Context:** Ted plays Rupert, the arrogant former club owner, in a pub darts match. The stakes: if Rupert wins, he picks Richmond's starting lineup; if Ted wins, Rupert is banned from the owner's box. On his final turn Ted needs two triple-twenties and a bullseye. He tells a story while he throws.

| Beat | What Ted does | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | Tells Rupert that men have underestimated him his whole life, and for years he never understood why | [[curiosity]] and [[tension]]: an open question |
| Concrete scene | Driving his son to school, he sees a quote attributed to Walt Whitman painted on a wall: "Be curious, not judgmental." | Zoom in on a moment |
| The unasked question | If they'd been curious, they'd have asked questions, like "Have you played a lot of darts, Ted?" | The question is posed; the answer is held back |
| Payoff | "Yes, sir. Every Sunday afternoon at a sports bar with my father from age 10 till I was 16, when he passed away." The final dart hits the bullseye. | Payoff at the moment of maximum impact |

**Why it works:**
- **Withholding the payoff.** Ted never opens by admitting he's an experienced darts player.
- **Tension through an unresolved question.** The whole story hangs on *why* he was underestimated.
- **The right amount of specificity.** He avoids both the vague version ("Yes, I played darts a lot growing up") and a flood of backstory. One sentence gives four concrete details (Sunday afternoons, a sports bar, his father, age 10 to 16), then his father's death for emotional weight. See [[right-amount-of-specificity]].
- **Competence and warmth in one reveal.** The same sentence shows mastery and grief. See [[competence-and-warmth]].

## Example 2: Workplace Openings

Jenkins translates the lesson to corporate settings. Instead of a helicopter summary, open inside a moment:

- *"Three weeks before launch, my biggest customer looked across the table at me and said, 'This is never going to work.'"*
- *"I'm sitting in a meeting when our sales director puts one number on the screen and he says, 'Guys, I think we've been solving for the wrong problem.'"*

Each line has a specific room, a line of dialogue, and immediate stakes. These are the most directly reusable examples in the source. See [[five-second-moment]] and [[cold-open]].

## Example 3: The Press Conference About Earl

**Context:** Earl, the team's beloved greyhound mascot, chases a pigeon onto the pitch during a penalty kick and is struck and killed by the ball. At the post-match press conference, journalist Trent Crimm asks Ted for a statement.

| Beat | What Ted does | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| Unexpected hook | Opens a tribute to a dog by saying that when he was 3, the neighbor's dog attacked him | Unexpected opening ([[surprise]], [[curiosity]]) |
| Lead to an assumption | Describes a childhood of being afraid of dogs. The audience assumes he dislikes dogs and maybe doesn't care about Earl | Forced reinterpretation: the setup |
| Fast-forward | In high school, the neighbor's wife dies and the neighbor neglects his dog | [[pacing]]: years in a couple of sentences |
| Twist | The neglected dog was Hank, the same dog that bit him. Ted starts feeding him, walking him, adopts him, and cares for him until he dies | Forced reinterpretation: the reveal. A story that seemed to be about indifference becomes a story about compassion |
| Meaning | The thing that once made him cry just knowing it existed became the thing that made him cry knowing it was gone. Those things come into our lives to help us get from one place to a better one | [[what-happened-vs-what-it-meant]] |
| Takeaway | He hopes they helped Earl do just that, and says they'll miss him | [[cart-framework]]: the meaning brought back to the room |

Jenkins calls the meaning beat "perhaps the most important part of the story."

## Do vs. Don't

### 1. Chronological setup vs. mystery hook
- **Don't:** *"When I was 10 years old, I used to play darts with my dad..."*
- **Do:** *"Rupert, guys have underestimated me my entire life, and for years I never understood why. It used to really bother me."*
- **Why:** Giving the background at the start kills curiosity. An unresolved vulnerability makes the listener lean in. → [[curiosity]], [[tension]]

> [!conflict]- Disputed: PAST says to state the where and when in the first sentence
> The PAST source's rule for openings (ground the scene with place and time first) produces something close to Jenkins's "Don't." See [[debate-how-to-open]].

### 2. Executive summary vs. a scene
- **Don't:** *"We realized something needed to change, there was a lot of resistance, but eventually we found a solution."*
- **Do:** *"Three weeks before launch, my biggest customer looked across the table at me and said, 'This is never going to work.'"*
- **Why:** Summaries report facts; a specific scene puts the listener in the room and makes the stakes immediate. → [[five-second-moment]]

### 3. Premature climax vs. controlled payoff
- **Don't:** *"I grew up playing darts with my father, so when this guy challenged me to a game, I decided to play dumb."*
- **Do:** Pose the missing question first (*"Have you played a lot of darts, Ted?"*), let it hang, and deliver the answer only on the final throw.
- **Why:** The weak version explains the trick before performing it. The controlled version makes the audience want the answer before giving it. → [[curiosity]]

> [!conflict]- Disputed: BLUF and Minto say to lead with the conclusion
> The BLUF source and Barbara Minto argue the answer should come first so the listener can act. See [[debate-answer-first-or-last]].

### 4. Vague or overloaded vs. the right amount of detail
- **Don't:** A vague summary (*"Yes, I played darts a lot growing up"*) or a sprawling backstory.
- **Do:** *"Every Sunday afternoon at a sports bar with my father from age 10 till I was 16, when he passed away."*
- **Why:** A specific day, place, age range, and loss give just enough anchors to picture the ritual instantly without slowing the story. → [[right-amount-of-specificity]]

### 5. Predictable path vs. forced reinterpretation
- **Don't:** A linear path where every detail confirms what the opening already suggested. (Jenkins implies this side rather than stating it.)
- **Do:** Lead the audience to assume Ted dislikes dogs, then reveal that the dog he nursed and loved was the one that attacked him.
- **Why:** The listener has to reprocess everything they just heard. Once the assumption collapses, they no longer know what comes next, so engagement spikes. → [[surprise]]

### 6. Uniform speed vs. varied pace
- **Don't:** Tell every stage at the same speed, giving bridge steps the same weight as turning points. (Implied by Jenkins.)
- **Do:** Slow down for the attack and the final reflection; compress years of caring for Hank into a few quick sentences.
- **Why:** Pace tells the audience what matters. Uniform pace tires listeners and dilutes the emotion. → [[pacing]]

### 7. What happened vs. what it meant
- **Don't:** *"And then a year later we had to put Hank to sleep, and that was that."*
- **Do:** Point out that the dog that once caused tears of fear became the dog that caused tears of grief, and that such things come into our lives to get us somewhere better.
- **Why:** Events alone are just history. Saying what they meant turns a personal memory into a message for the listener. → [[what-happened-vs-what-it-meant]]

## Key Terms

- **Helicopter view:** Summarizing events in broad abstractions ("there was resistance") instead of showing the concrete moment it happened.
- **Right amount of specificity:** Enough concrete detail to see and feel the moment, no more. → [[right-amount-of-specificity]]
- **Forced reinterpretation:** Leading the listener toward a plausible assumption, then revealing something that makes them re-evaluate everything before it. → [[surprise]]
- **Personal vs. private:** Personal details reveal values, rituals, and emotions; private details are unnecessary intimate oversharing. → [[competence-and-warmth]]
- **CART:** Context, Action, Result, Takeaway. → [[cart-framework]]

## Claims to Verify

- **"Be curious, not judgmental" attributed to Walt Whitman.** Needs verification. The line is widely considered a misattribution. Note that the misattribution doesn't weaken the story, which is a live example of [[truth-vs-facts]].
