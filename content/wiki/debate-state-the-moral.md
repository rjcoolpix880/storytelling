# Debate: Should You Say What the Story Meant?

**Summary**: The PAST source says announcing the moral kills a story; Shannon Jenkins says stating what the story meant is its most important step.

**Lenses**: [[storytelling]]

**Sources**: [Give me 18min & I'll improve your storytelling skills by 183%](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YtkrIaONxu0), [How to Tell Stories Better Than 99% of People](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lghSjBl9yGM), [Speak Better Than 99% of People: A Practical Guide to Storytelling](https://www.shannonjenkins.co/)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-25

**Type**: Direct contradiction

---

## The question

When a story ends, should the teller spell out its meaning, or stop and let the story speak for itself?

## Position 1: PAST source — don't state the moral; stop

"The moral of the story is..." is one of six phrases that kill a story. The fix: **end on your final Thought or transformation, and then stop talking.**

**Why:** Explaining the point tells the listener what to feel and robs them of the experience the story was building. If the story worked, the point is already made. See [[story-killers]].

**Example:**
- **Don't:** "...and the moral of the story is..."
- **Do:** End on the raw final thought from the moment of change, then stop.

## Position 2: Shannon Jenkins — land with a takeaway

Don't leave the audience wondering why you told the story. Tell them what it *meant*. The Takeaway is the part of her [[cart-framework]] that communicators most often leave out, and she calls the meaning "perhaps the most important part of the story." It's the move that turns a personal memory into a message for the listener. See [[what-happened-vs-what-it-meant]].

**Why:** Events by themselves are just history. A leader tells a story to make a point, and an audience that doesn't get the point got nothing.

In her written guide (see [[speak-better-than-99-percent]]), the takeaway follows from a rule set before the story is even chosen: **"The story serves the message."** Never leave people wondering, "Why did you tell me that?" See [[choosing-the-right-story]].

**Example (from the guide):**
- **Avoid:** "That was a really important experience for me."
- **Try:** "That experience taught me that if we wait until an opportunity feels completely safe and obvious, we've probably waited too long."

**Example (Ted Lasso on Hank):**
- **Don't:** "And then a year later we had to put Hank to sleep, and that was that."
- **Do:** The thing that once made him cry just knowing it existed became the thing that made him cry knowing it was gone. Such things come into our lives to get us from one place to a better one. He hopes they helped Earl do just that.

## Not a party to this debate

The [[five-line-story]] source says only that the Result should satisfy the audience and "tie everything together." It takes no position on stating the meaning.

## The wiki's view (optional)

*This is synthesis, not a verdict.*

- **Voice may matter more than the act.** Ted never says "the moral is." He delivers the meaning as his own realization, in the moment, which is arguably a final *Thought* in PAST's own terms. What PAST targets may be the lecture framing more than the meaning itself.
- **Context differs.** The PAST source is about personal storytelling, where the experience is the point. Jenkins is about leaders telling stories in meetings to make a point.
- The disagreement doesn't fully go away, though. PAST says stop at the transformation; Jenkins's example keeps going to a broader lesson and then applies it to the room.
