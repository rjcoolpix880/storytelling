# Debate: Answer First, or Make Them Wait?

**Summary**: BLUF and Minto say to lead with the conclusion; Van Neistat and Shannon Jenkins say giving the key fact first ruins a story. Jenkins herself leads with the point outside of stories.

**Lenses**: [[storytelling]], [[executive]]

**Sources**: [Why Most Tech Professionals Are Terrible Communicators](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kdy-C61fb5g&t=1s), [The Minto Pyramid Principle Explained (With Example Frameworks)](https://www.betterup.com/blog/minto-pyramid), [Van Neistat's Guide to Master Storytelling](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DyyjXFgM_6k), [How to Tell Stories Better Than 99% of People](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lghSjBl9yGM), [The Three Frameworks You Need to Create Powerful Presentations and Tell Compelling Stories](https://tomtunguz.com/storytelling-duarte/), [Monroe's Motivated Sequence | Public Speaking](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/wm-publicspeaking/chapter/monroes-motivated-sequence/), [Speak Better Than 99% of People: A Practical Guide to Storytelling](https://www.shannonjenkins.co/), [Speak Like a Leader](mailto:hello@shannonjenkins.co)

## The question

Should you give the audience the conclusion up front, or build to it?

This disagreement comes from different lenses. BLUF and Minto write from the [[executive]] lens, where a busy listener needs the answer to decide or act. Van Neistat and Jenkins write from the [[storytelling]] lens, where the withheld payoff is what keeps the audience listening.

## Position 1: BLUF source — lead with the destination

Most professionals communicate bottom-up: the investigation, the data, the discussions, and the conclusion last. By then the listener has lost the thread. Flip it: lead with the conclusion and the ask, then offer the supporting journey. Leading with the headline invites the listener to ask questions on their own terms. See [[bluf-method]].

**Example:**
- **Don't:** Recount the codebase history and deadline pressures before landing on the problem.
- **Do:** "Feature delivery has slowed down because we've accumulated too much technical debt, especially in the authentication module... I'd like to propose pausing one feature track next quarter."

## Position 2: Barbara Minto — bottom line first

State the overall argument or recommendation first, then key points, then data. Built for executives with no time to read walls of text (79% of people scan rather than read). Stating the conclusion first also frames every later detail around it. See [[minto-pyramid-principle]].

## Position 3: Van Neistat — leading with the key fact is the amateur's mistake

The amateur opens with the key fact (Whitney Houston was in the waiting room) and then adds detail. The master builds tension first and saves the key fact for the end. See [[van-neistat-storytelling]].

## Position 4: Shannon Jenkins — withhold the payoff

Give the audience what they need to keep following, but don't give them the payoff until you're ready. See [[curiosity]] and [[how-to-tell-stories-better]].

**Example (darts):**
- **Don't:** "I grew up playing darts with my father, so when this guy challenged me to a game, I decided to play dumb."
- **Do:** Pose the question ("Have you played a lot of darts, Ted?"), let it hang, and answer only on the final throw.

In her written guide (see [[speak-better-than-99-percent]]), she puts it as: give people what they need to follow you, "but save the payoff until it has earned its moment." The same guide also says to decide the message *before* choosing a story (see [[choosing-the-right-story]]), so for her the point comes first in preparation, and last in the telling.

## Jenkins on both sides

In her email on executive presence (see [[speak-like-a-leader]]), Jenkins gives the answer-first advice for meetings. When someone asks a simple question, don't give them the full history: "lead with your main point, then add only the context people need to understand it." For a decision: "There are three options. I recommend B, and here's why."

So one author holds Position 4 for stories and Positions 1 and 2 for answers and status updates. She doesn't present this as a contradiction, and the sources don't explain where the line falls. See [[executive-presence]].

## Structures that build before the answer

Two more sources don't argue the point directly, but their structures put the answer after the build-up:
- **Duarte's SCQA story template** runs Situation → Complication → Question → Answer. (Minto uses the same four beats but places the Answer first. The same scaffold is used both ways.) See [[scqa-framework]].
- **Monroe** establishes the Need before offering the Satisfaction. See [[monroes-motivated-sequence]].

## The wiki's view (optional)

*This is synthesis, not a verdict.*

- The answer-first sources are about **decisions and work communication**: the listener needs the answer in order to act. The build-up sources are about **stories and persuasion**: the payoff is the reward, or the audience has to feel the need before the answer lands. Hence the label "different context."
- The two can nest: open a meeting with the bottom line, then tell a story inside it that withholds its own payoff.
- Jenkins's split supports the "different context" label: the same author gives both answers, depending on whether the listener asked a question or is hearing a story.
- Answer-first creates its own kind of curiosity: a clear headline raises "why?" and "how?"
