# The Expected Answer Trap

**Summary**: Answering a brief exactly as asked gives the same answer everyone else will give. Look for the less obvious, smarter angle, often what the audience actually wants rather than the technology or features they named.

**Lenses**: [[executive]], [[technical]]

**Sources**: [How to Sell Your Ideas Better Than 99% of Your Colleagues](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kYv9N8F7l5o)

**Last updated**: 2026-10-21

---

Shannon Jenkins's first lesson from Don Draper's Kodak pitch (see [[how-to-sell-your-ideas]]): don't automatically give people the answer they expect.

## The Trap

If a client gives you a brief, they've probably given it to your competitors too. Answer it literally and your pitch is identical to everyone else's: a "race to the middle." Kodak expected a pitch about innovation and "the wheel" (the projector's circular tray). That's what they asked for. It was the expected answer, not the effective one.

- **Don't:** Answer the brief literally and give the client exactly what they asked for ("reinventing the wheel").
- **Do:** Realize every competitor is giving the same answer, and pivot to a deeper, less obvious angle (nostalgia).
- **Why:** It avoids the race to the middle and offers something nobody else will.

## The "Glittering Lure"

Don's line: "Technology is a glittering lure. But there's the rare occasion when the public can be engaged on a level beyond flash if they have a sentimental bond with the product."

Jenkins's lesson: technical specs and "innovation" are often distractions. The job is to work out what the audience actually craves (here, sentiment), not just what they say they want (technology).

**For technical communicators (wiki synthesis):** this is a pitch-level version of advice the technical lens already gives. Features are the complete answer; what the features mean to the listener is the useful one. See [[useful-vs-complete]] and [[jit-context]]. Rob Willis's Knowledge Deficit Trap (over-explaining to sound smart) is the same lure seen from the speaker's side. See [[sounding-smart]].

## The Exercise: Find the Smarter Answer

Before building a pitch, ask:

> *Is there a less obvious but smarter answer? Do I see something in the problem that my client or boss may have missed?*

Or, in Jenkins's summary: "Ask what everyone else is likely to say and see whether you can see something that they have missed."

## How This Relates to Other Pages

- **[[surprise]].** The expected answer is the pitch version of a report: nothing in it the audience didn't see coming. A less obvious angle gives a pitch the gap between expectation and reality that makes a story work.
- **[[choosing-the-right-story]].** Jenkins's storytelling advice says decide the message first. This lesson adds a check on the message itself: is it the one everyone else would choose?
- **[[sounding-smart]] (a nuance).** Willis says to "speak the audience's language," the decision-maker's own words. Don ignores the client's framing ("the wheel," "technology"). The wiki's reading: these aren't opposed. Don rejects the brief's *angle*, but speaks directly to a feeling the audience already has.
- **[[what-is-vs-what-could-be]].** The smarter answer often changes what the "what could be" is. Kodak's future was a better gadget; Don's was a way back to the people you love.
