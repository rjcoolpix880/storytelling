# Debate: Where Does the Surprise Go?

**Summary**: Van Neistat says the surprise is saved for the end; Shannon Jenkins also uses the unexpected at the start and in the middle of a story.

**Lenses**: [[storytelling]]

**Sources**: [Van Neistat's Guide to Master Storytelling](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DyyjXFgM_6k), [How to Tell Stories Better Than 99% of People](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lghSjBl9yGM), [Speak Better Than 99% of People: A Practical Guide to Storytelling](https://www.shannonjenkins.co/)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-25

**Type**: Different emphasis

## The question

Does the surprise belong only at the end of a story, or can it land earlier?

## Position 1: Van Neistat — save the surprise for the end

A story must contain a surprise, and the master saves it (the "punchline") for the end, after building tension and emotional stakes. The amateur leads with the key fact and loses the effect. See [[van-neistat-storytelling]] and [[surprise]].

**Example (the Bert Kreischer story):**
- **Amateur:** Opens with "Isla was in the hospital and Whitney Houston was in the waiting room," then walks through boring details of the hospital visit.
- **Master:** Bert's baby daughter is in the hospital, he's distraught, he goes back to the waiting room, a woman tells him "It's tough being a daddy." And that woman was Whitney Houston.

## Position 2: Shannon Jenkins — the unexpected can open the story and turn it mid-way

Jenkins's fifth lesson is to introduce something unexpected: "a twist, a surprising detail, or an unexpected opening." Her main example places the twist in the *middle*: **lead the audience toward a reasonable assumption, then reveal something that forces them to reinterpret everything they just heard.** The twist restarts curiosity, and the story continues to a final beat of meaning. She also uses the unexpected at the *start*: an opening far from what the audience expects. See [[how-to-tell-stories-better]].

**Example (Ted Lasso on Earl):**
- **Unexpected opening:** Asked about the death of Earl the dog, Ted opens with being attacked by a dog at age 3.
- **Mid-story twist:** After describing his fear of dogs, he reveals that the neglected dog he later fed, walked, and adopted was Hank, the same dog that bit him.
- **Ending:** Not a new fact but the meaning: what Hank's life and death taught him.

Her written guide (see [[speak-better-than-99-percent]]) states the principle behind this: "Predictability kills attention. A surprising opening, detail, decision or twist makes people mentally re-engage." Among the places to look for it: "new information changed the meaning of what came before."

## Where they agree

Both say to withhold the payoff and control what the listener knows and when. Jenkins's darts story saves its payoff (Ted's history with his father) for the final throw, exactly as Van Neistat would.

## The wiki's view (optional)

*This is synthesis, not a verdict.*

A story can have more than one reveal. Van Neistat's rule may be best read as "save the *final* surprise for the end." Jenkins adds that an early or mid-story surprise does a different job: it creates or restarts curiosity instead of resolving it.
