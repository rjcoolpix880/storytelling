# Truth vs. Facts

**Summary**: The principle that emotional resonance and human truth matter more than strict factual accuracy — minor facts may flex to preserve a story's essential reality.

**Lenses**: [[storytelling]]

**Sources**: [Van Neistat's Guide to Master Storytelling](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DyyjXFgM_6k), [Give me 18min & I'll improve your storytelling skills by 183%](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YtkrIaONxu0), [How to Tell Stories Better Than 99% of People](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lghSjBl9yGM)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-25

---

> "Never let the facts get in the way of the truth."

## What This Means

A story can be factually imprecise and still be deeply true. The "truth" of a story is its emotional core — the human experience it captures. A master storyteller may adjust minor details (exact chronology, specific words spoken, peripheral facts) when those details would interrupt or diminish the emotional impact.

This is not fabrication. The essential facts — what happened, who was affected, what changed — must be real. What can flex are details that carry no emotional weight but could disrupt the story's flow.

## How This Appears in the Sources

The [[past-storytelling-technique]] source touches on this in the Speech pillar: "You can reconstruct dialogue from memory — precision matters less than immediacy." Van Neistat makes the principle broader and explicit: it applies to any minor detail, not just dialogue.

## Distinction from Useful vs. Complete

[[Useful-vs-complete]] and truth-vs-facts are related but address different problems:

| Concept | The tension | The resolution |
|---|---|---|
| [[useful-vs-complete]] | Complete information vs. useful information | Give what the listener needs; omit the rest |
| Truth vs. Facts | Factual accuracy vs. emotional truth | Preserve the truth; let minor facts flex |

Useful-vs-complete is about *how much* to say. Truth-vs-facts is about *how precisely to say it*. Both resolve in favor of the listener's experience over the speaker's desire for completeness or accuracy.

## Why It Matters

When you interrupt a good story to correct an irrelevant fact, you rob the listener of the experience the story was building toward. The fact may be accurate; the damage to the story is real. Emotional truth is what creates connection — see [[van-neistat-storytelling]] on story as human connection. It is also what makes [[surprise]] land.

## An Example: The Whitman Quote

In Shannon Jenkins's darts example (see [[how-to-tell-stories-better]]), Ted Lasso credits "Be curious, not judgmental" to Walt Whitman. That attribution is widely considered wrong (needs verification). The story works anyway, because the attribution carries no emotional weight. What matters is the idea and the moment Ted saw it. This is the principle in action: a minor fact is off, and the truth is intact.

A nuance, which is a wiki synthesis: this flex is safest for minor details of your *own* experience (exact words, exact dates). A misattributed quote or a wrong statistic in a professional talk is something the audience can check, and getting it wrong can cost credibility, the competence half of [[competence-and-warmth]]. Let the details of your memory flex; check the claims.

## Specificity Without Precision

[[right-amount-of-specificity]] asks for concrete details (a day, a place, a person), and truth-vs-facts allows those details to be imperfect. The two fit together: a detail's job is to help the listener see and feel the moment, not to serve as a record.

## A Related Distinction: Belief vs. Confidence

[[belief-vs-confidence]] applies a similar authenticity-over-precision logic to the *speaker* rather than the story's content: a speaker's genuine conviction persuades more than polished delivery, the way emotional truth persuades more than factual precision. See that page for the full comparison.
