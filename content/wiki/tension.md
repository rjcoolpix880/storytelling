# Tension

**Summary**: A reason to care what happens next: something uncertain plus something at stake. It doesn't need drama. At work, a small unresolved question with real consequences is enough.

**Lenses**: [[storytelling]]

**Sources**: [How to Tell Stories Better Than 99% of People](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lghSjBl9yGM), [The ONLY 5 Lines You Need To Tell Any Story](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NeTJRCycYXQ), [Van Neistat's Guide to Master Storytelling](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DyyjXFgM_6k), [Speak Better Than 99% of People: A Practical Guide to Storytelling](https://www.shannonjenkins.co/)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-25

---

Tension is Shannon Jenkins's second lesson (see [[how-to-tell-stories-better]]): give the audience a reason to care about what happens next. She names three ingredients: something **uncertain**, something **important at stake**, and something the listener **wants resolved**.

## Tension Doesn't Require Drama

Jenkins's key point for professional settings: tension doesn't mean somebody is about to die or the company is about to collapse. It means planting a question the audience wants answered. A product launch, a skeptical customer, or one surprising number on a slide can carry enough tension for a meeting.

- **Don't:** *"We realized something needed to change, there was a lot of resistance, but eventually we found a solution."* The outcome is given away and the stakes are abstract.
- **Do:** *"Three weeks before launch, my biggest customer looked across the table at me and said, 'This is never going to work.'"* A deadline (uncertain), the biggest customer (stakes), and no resolution yet.

In her written guide (see [[speak-better-than-99-percent]]), Jenkins labels this pair **Reporting vs. Story** and shortens the Don't to one line: *"There was a lot of resistance to the launch."* She defines tension there simply as uncertainty, "something your audience now wants resolved," and lists the kind of questions it plants:

- *Will this work?*
- *Will the client say yes?*
- *Were we wrong?*
- *What happens next?*

## The Nuance: Tension vs. Curiosity

The two overlap, and Jenkins's own definition of tension uses the word "curiosity." The wiki separates them:

- **[[curiosity]]** is about *knowing*: "Why? What's the connection?" It can exist with nothing at stake, like a riddle.
- **Tension** is about *caring how it turns out*: "Will it work out?" It needs stakes. It can exist with no mystery at all, as in a crisis where everyone knows what's happening but not how it ends.

The darts scene has both. Curiosity: why was Ted always underestimated? Tension: he needs three perfect darts, and Rupert gets to control the team if he misses.

## Where Tension Comes From

- **Desire plus conflict.** In the [[five-line-story]], [[desire]] names what someone wants, and Conflict threatens it. "Stories without conflict have nothing at stake." Desire gives tension its stakes; conflict gives it uncertainty.
- **The gap in persuasion.** In presentations, tension is the gap between the present and a better future. See [[what-is-vs-what-could-be]]. The Complication in [[scqa-framework]] and the Need step in [[monroes-motivated-sequence]] are tension built into an argument.
- **Building stakes before the reveal.** Van Neistat's master storyteller builds tension and emotional stakes first and only then lands the [[surprise]]. See [[van-neistat-storytelling]].

## Holding and Releasing Tension

- **Don't release it early.** Explaining the outcome up front ends the tension. See the premature-climax contrast in [[how-to-tell-stories-better]].
- **Slow down where tension peaks.** [[pacing]] stretches the moments that matter. Ted doesn't hurry the final throw.
- **Release it deliberately.** A surprise or payoff releases tension; the takeaway then tells the audience what the release meant. See [[what-happened-vs-what-it-meant]].
- **Sustain it across a whole talk.** Duarte's oscillation between what is and what could be keeps reopening the gap so tension doesn't drain away. See [[duarte-resonate]].

## A Caution: Don't Inflate the Stakes

This is a wiki synthesis, not a point from the sources. Because tension only needs real, modest stakes, exaggerating them isn't necessary and can backfire. [[truth-vs-facts]] allows minor details to flex, but the essential facts (what happened and what was at risk) have to be real. Overstated stakes also cost credibility, which is part of [[competence-and-warmth]].
