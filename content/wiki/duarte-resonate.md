# Duarte's Resonate — Three Frameworks for Presentations

**Summary**: Nancy Duarte's three-part toolkit for building persuasive presentations — the Audience's Journey, the SCQA story template, and the Sparkline — synthesized in Tomasz Tunguz's review of her book *Resonate*.

**Sources**: [The Three Frameworks You Need to Create Powerful Presentations and Tell Compelling Stories](https://tomtunguz.com/storytelling-duarte/)

**Last updated**: 2026-05-28

---

A persuasive presenter convinces the audience to believe in a new idea. To do that, they must contrast the ordinary world of today with the special world of the future — and lead the audience across the gap.

Duarte gives presenters three tools to do this work.

## 1. The Audience's Journey

A 12-step adaptation of the hero's journey, but with the audience as protagonist. The first four steps:

1. Paint the ordinary world (the present)
2. Contrast it with the special world (the future)
3. Issue the call to adventure
4. Handle the audience's immediate objections
5. Establish the presenter as the mentor

The remaining steps mirror the rest of the classic hero's arc. Duarte demonstrates the parallel by mapping the journey onto Luke Skywalker in Star Wars. See [[audience-journey]] for the full concept.

## 2. The Story Template — SCQA

A MadLibs-style fill-in-the-blank scaffold for the arc of the talk: Situation, Complication, Question, Answer. The same four beats Barbara Minto uses inside [[minto-pyramid-principle]]. Two independent sources converging on this structure suggests it is doing real work.

Duarte's warning: complexity kills presentations. The template forces simplification. See [[scqa-framework]] for the full concept.

## 3. The Sparkline

A visual diagram of the presentation over time. Two axes:

- **X-axis**: time through the talk
- **Y-axis**: whether the presenter is describing the present ("what is") or the future ("what could be")

A great presentation oscillates between the two. The audience's claps, laughs, and moments of wonder line the bottom of the chart, showing where the oscillation lands emotionally.

Duarte applies sparklines to Steve Jobs's iPhone announcement, Feynman's lecture on gravity, Benjamin Zander's TED talk on music, Martin Luther King's "I Have a Dream," and Ronald Reagan's Challenger Disaster speech. The common pattern: sustained oscillation between [[what-is-vs-what-could-be]] is what drives the audience to share the presenter's vision.

The sparkline doubles as a planning tool (design the oscillation in advance) and a feedback loop (review the rhythm after delivering).

## The Core Mechanic

All three frameworks point to the same underlying claim: **persuasion lives in the gap between the present and the future, and the presenter's job is to sustain that gap throughout the talk.**

- The Audience's Journey names the gap as the dramatic structure
- SCQA names it as the logical structure (Complication → Answer)
- The Sparkline names it as the rhythmic structure

This is the same mechanism Monroe's Need → Visualize pair drives in [[monroes-motivated-sequence]], stretched across an entire presentation.

## How It Connects to Other Frameworks

- The "what is / what could be" oscillation is the most reusable insight from this source — see [[what-is-vs-what-could-be]].
- The audience-as-hero framing inverts a common presenter mistake: making the presenter (or product) the hero. The presenter is the *mentor*, not the protagonist.
- "Complexity kills presentations" parallels [[useful-vs-complete]] and [[story-killers]] — overload defeats the message.
- The sparkline's emphasis on emotional response (claps, laughs, wonder) connects to [[surprise]]: the master measures not what was said but what the audience *felt*.
